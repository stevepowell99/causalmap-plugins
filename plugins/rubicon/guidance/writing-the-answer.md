# Writing the answer

The answer is read by the evaluator who asked the question, an intelligent professional in their field, and by whoever they pass the report to. Most readers stop early, so write and check it from the top down.

## The order of priority

Each part is held to a higher standard than the part after it. Where two parts compete for a point, the earlier one gets what the reader most needs and the later one gets the rest.

- **The title** names what the answer is about in the evaluator's own terms, and states the finding where a short phrase can carry it.
- **The first sentence** answers the question, or its first question, directly, with any number and what it is out of.
- **The first paragraph** gives the whole answer: what each question asked comes to, the main reason, and how sure it is. A reader who stops here has the answer and has not been told anything the rest takes back.
- **The first section** sets out the evidence for the finding the answer most turns on.
- **The rest** carries the other findings, the accounts that go against them, and the limitations.

A qualifier, or an account that goes against a finding, belongs where that finding is set out in full. It goes in the title, the first sentence or the first paragraph only where it changes the answer itself.

A summary says less than what it summarises, never more. Where the title, the first sentence or the first paragraph cannot carry a qualifier, narrow the claim instead: "two of the three", "most of the small grantees", "fairly sure".

## Say each thing once

- State each finding, qualifier and account against a finding in one place. The opening and the start of each section point to the finding; they do not repeat its detail.
- A section starts with its point, not with a restatement of the question or of what the previous section said.
- The limitations hold what changes what the reader should believe and is not said elsewhere.
- Cut what does not bear on the question, and do not narrate the method beyond what the reader needs to weigh a finding.

## Plain, for a professional

- Use the shortest word that is still exact, and the ordinary vocabulary of the evaluator's field. A term outside that field gets a plain phrase on first use.
- Match the English of the question, British or American.
- Every number says what it counts and out of what before the reader meets it, in the sentence or in a table's headers.
- Negate the verb: "the coordinator did not reply to any of them", not "the coordinator gave no reply".
- Every pronoun points at one thing. Where "they", "it" or "which" could refer to two nouns, repeat the noun.
- Write a date with the month as a word, never as numbers alone.
- Name a section or a finding rather than referring to it by its number or position.
- Report what was done, plainly. Do not argue for the design or answer objections nobody has made.
- Nest a bullet under another only where it qualifies, limits or gives an example of it.

## What reads as machine-written

Replace each of these with the plain fact it stood in for, or cut it.

- Praise for the programme, the people or the analysis: powerful, robust, comprehensive, seamless, rich, key.
- Stock words: delve, deep dive, shape (as a verb), landscape, tapestry, nuanced, crucial, notably, honest, genuine.
- Lists of three where the material has two or four.
- Strings of clauses joined by ", and".
- Paragraphs that all open the same way, and a closing sentence that sums up the paragraph it ends.
- A dash used to join two clauses that a full stop or a colon would join.
