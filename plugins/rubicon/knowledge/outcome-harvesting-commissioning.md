# What commissioners ask a harvest, and what to answer

What commissioners ask an outcome harvest, taken from the terms of reference of three published harvests, which of those questions to refuse, and what to offer instead. The other pages on outcome harvesting say how to do it; this one says what people buy it for, taken from the terms of reference of published harvests rather than from method guides, because what a method is for and what it gets bought for are different things.

Back to [outcome harvesting](outcome-harvesting.md). The analysis machinery is on [answering questions with a harvest](outcome-harvesting-analysis.md); this page sits in front of it and decides which question is being answered at all.

## The nine questions, and what is missing from them

Three published harvests, nine commissioned questions between them.

**Oxfam Novib, global programme, Wilson-Grau's own harvest.** To what extent counterparts achieved outcomes and contributed to policy and practice changes. Whether the programme responded effectively to a changing global context. Whether counterparts added value to Oxfam's campaigning. And "How has GloPro contributed to the achievements of outcomes by counterparts".

**NIMD, across Mali, Mozambique, Tunisia and Colombia.** Whether the outcomes "represent patterns of progress towards their respective programme objectives". How well the outcomes "match country-level ToCs". How suitable outcome harvesting was as a method.

**ActionAid Denmark, Tax Justice programme.** Six, one of which the report says may not be answerable by an outcome evaluation at all.

**Not one of the nine asks how much of the change was attributable to the funder.** That is worth knowing before refusing the question, because it means the refusal is usually a correction to one commissioner rather than a fight with the field.

Five recurring shapes:

- **What changed, in actors we do not control, including what nobody planned?** The harvest itself, and the thing OH does that no other method here does.
- **Do these outcomes form a pattern of progress towards the objectives?**
- **How well do the outcomes match the theory of change?**
- **How did we contribute?**
- **Was this method any good here?** Asked more often than you would expect, and answerable.

The second and third are analysis questions asked of a table that already exists. They are also the thinnest area of published guidance and the place most harvests disappoint the people who paid, which makes them the ones worth building for.

## Refusing is normal, and there is a house style for it

All three reports refuse something in public, which is the model to follow.

- Oxfam's evaluators **rewrote a sub-question from the terms of reference**, saying the original wording "is misleading because we agreed in the Approach paper that we would not make a comparison between planned outcomes and the outcomes actually achieved", and that they had corrected it.
- NIMD's evaluators **refused a judgement of worth**: "we cannot draw conclusions about the significance of what was achieved ... since we are assessing the achievement of programmatic objectives but not the merit or value of those changes."
- ActionAid's report **declines to make recommendations**, citing Wilson-Grau and Britt that an outcome harvest evaluator can rarely recommend action because too many determining factors are unknown to them, and offers discussion points instead.

The pattern in each: name the question as asked, say what was answered instead, say why, in the report rather than in a conversation nobody minuted.

## The four questions to refuse, and what to offer

- **How much of the change was ours?** No harvest supports a fraction. Offer instead: for each outcome, what the actor and an independent substantiator each say the programme had to do with it.
- **Compared with what would have happened anyway?** There is no counterfactual and no comparison group. Offer instead: the rival explanations named and searched for.
- **Is it value for money?** Needs a denominator and a valuation, and a harvest has neither. Offer instead: the outcomes, with their significance ratings, as one input to somebody else's judgement.
- **How representative is this of everything that happened?** The hardest, because the true answer sounds evasive. See below.

## The denominator problem, in the practitioners' own words

A harvest is a search rather than a sample, so a count from it has no base. The published reports say so more plainly than most secondary accounts.

Oxfam: the outcomes "are not exhaustive. They are a sampling of what the 38 counterparts consider to be amongst their ten most significant outcomes", with a warning that "Caution should be taken ... in making quantitative comparisons and contrasts between outcomes".

ActionAid is the more useful case, because it shows what replaces sampling. Representativeness is claimed through a **gap-filling round**: at the end of the harvesting workshop the participants read the whole set, named change areas they thought missing or under-represented, and added outcomes in those areas the following week. The report also flags two biases in the counts, that most outcomes are recent, and that a first harvest skews large, only 6 per cent being rated minor.

So the answer to "how representative is this" is a description of the search and its gap-filling, never a proportion.

## One harvest that reported a contribution percentage

Worth knowing because it contradicts the orthodoxy this page has just stated. ActionAid asked change agents to **rate their own contribution to each outcome as a percentage**, recorded it as a formal classification field beside year, country, social actor and significance, and produced a number of outcomes by contribution percentage. Ratings clustered above 60 per cent, nineteen outcomes at 80 and three at 90. The evaluator adjusted percentages down where documentation was thin, and substantiators rated independently: of eighteen substantiated outcomes only three were rated lower than the change agent's own number, the largest gap 90 against 50.

So refusing the attribution question is a position practitioners take, not a rule the method enforces. Where a commissioner insists, this is what they get, and its provenance has to travel with the number: a self-rating by an interested party, adjusted by an evaluator, checked by a substantiator that party nominated.

## What substantiation actually changes

Oxfam substantiated every other outcome: 112 attempted, 95 people responded covering 66 outcomes, 30 fully substantiated, 21 partially, 12 mixed. **Three drew a disagreement.** Forty-six attempts failed because no substantiator could be reached.

Two readings and the data cannot separate them. Either the descriptions were accurate, or substantiation rarely overturns anything because the substantiator is nominated by the person being checked. The number that would settle it, how often a substantiator nominated by somebody else disagrees, is not reported anywhere found.

Tell a commissioner what substantiation buys: corroboration by a named third party, not independent verification. And record the nomination, because it is what the reading turns on.

## What it costs the people in it

Oxfam's evaluators corresponded with each counterpart, typically two or three rounds, until they agreed a title, what changed, when and where, its significance and the contribution. They budgeted 11.5 hours per counterpart on average, and report that **some counterparts spent two to four times as long on it as the evaluators did**.

That is the true cost of a participatory method, and it falls mostly on people who are not being paid to evaluate. Any proposal to speed a harvest up with a machine should say whose time it saves. Drafting outcome statements faster saves the evaluator's.

## As a plan

The division stays the same: drafting outcome statements and running the substantiation loop stay human work the engine does not touch, and what a workflow does is the two analysis questions commissioners actually ask and existing guidance answers thinnest.

The harvested set, once drafted and substantiated by people, is read as the corpus, one outcome statement per document, or one row per statement in a table read as a document. A code step reads each statement against a column whose values are the programme's stated objectives, built from the theory of change and declared in full before any reading, each with its `means` and what does and does not count towards it; the coder records which objective or objectives a statement advances, or none. Because the objective list is fixed and complete before any reading, a tabulate step counting statements by objective prints every objective, with a count of none included, which is the gap-finding the method's own gap-filling workshop otherwise does by hand. The same tabulate step, or a second one `by` actor type or by year as well, gives outcomes per objective split however the question needs, and the objectives with nothing recorded against them stand out as zero cells rather than disappearing, which is the harder of the two questions and the one an evaluator cannot easily get any other way.

Every cell states plainly that its base is the harvested set and not a wider population, since a harvest is a search rather than a sample and nothing about tabulating it changes that. Where a commissioner presses for an attribution percentage, a value-for-money verdict or a claim about representativeness, a workflow refuses in the same terms the published harvests already use: `cannot_answer` names the part, says why, and names what should answer it instead, offering what the tables can support, each outcome's contribution claim with its claimant, or a description of the search and the gap-filling round, rather than a proportion.

The write step reports the pattern of progress as a proposal the evaluator can dispute rather than a finding stated as settled, since the sense-making stays a human workshop by design; its `review` setting (off by default) adds a second reading of the draft against the same tables and rows. Running the objective coding on a handful of already-harvested outcomes before the rest, within the budget the workflow is priced at, is mainly how an objective worded so broadly that every outcome advances it gets caught before it is applied to the whole set.
