# Coverage, sampling, and who does the harvesting

How an outcome harvest searches rather than samples, its five biases, when to stop, and working with programme staff as harvesters: two topics that belong together, because both determine what ends up in the set before anybody analyses it.

Back to [outcome harvesting](outcome-harvesting.md).

## A harvest is a search

This distinction runs through everything else on this page. A sample draws from a known population, so what you find supports inference about what you did not look at. A search finds what it finds. Nothing about the unsearched material follows from it.

So the quality of an outcome set is the quality of the search, and the report has to describe the search rather than the sample. Which documents were read. Which people were asked. Over what period. Who declined or never replied. A reader who cannot see the search cannot weigh anything in the set.

Where a proportion is wanted at analysis time, the search has to be turned into a census over a bounded frame. That decision belongs in step 1. See [the denominator problem](outcome-harvesting-analysis.md#the-denominator-problem).

## The five biases, and what to do about each

Every one of these pushes the set in the same direction: towards recent, visible, nearby, positive change described by articulate people.

**Recency.** Informants remember the last three months. Countermeasure: walk the period in named chunks rather than asking about "the project". "Let us take 2024 first. What changed that year?" Then 2025. Sampling documents evenly across the period does the same job for the written record.

**Documentation.** Formal changes leave a paper trail. A district team rearranging its own workflow leaves none. Countermeasure: weight interview effort towards the actors whose changes would never be written down, and expect the document pass to be dominated by policy and formal-act outcomes. Say this in the coverage paragraph, because it is the single biggest reason an outcome set skews towards the visible.

**Proximity.** Changes close to the programme get found. Second and third order changes do not, because nobody in the programme knows about them. Countermeasure: budget explicitly for interviews one and two removes out. Make "who else changed what they do because of something a partner did" a standing question. This is the countermeasure most often cut for budget. Cutting it removes the method's main advantage.

**Positivity.** Staff report good news, and partners funded by the programme report good news about the programme. Countermeasure: a fixed negative-outcome question asked of every informant, more than once, in different words. Treat a wholly positive set as evidence of search failure rather than of programme success. Write that judgement into the report.

**Articulacy.** An informant who tells a good story yields four statements; an equally important one who does not yields one. Countermeasure: count actors rather than statements in any reach claim, then go back to the less forthcoming informants with specific prompts drawn from what others said.

## Deciding when to stop

Saturation is the wrong idea here. New actors keep producing new outcomes because there is no population to saturate; you are simply meeting more people. A harvest that stops when informants stop yielding new material has stopped at the point the harvester ran out of contacts.

Stop on a declared rule instead, decided in step 1:

- Every document in a defined set has been read, and
- Every person on a defined informant list has been asked, or has declined twice, or
- A stated budget of harvester days has been spent.

Then report the rule and what was left undone. "We read all 43 quarterly and annual reports for 2023 to 2025, and interviewed 28 of the 35 people on the informant list; 7 did not respond after two approaches" is a coverage statement. "Data collection continued until saturation" is not, and in this method it is close to meaningless.

## Sampling within a harvest

Three separate sampling decisions, often confused with each other.

**Which sources to read.** A defined document set, a defined informant list. Where the corpus is too large to read, sample it, and say how. Random with a seed is preferable where any proportion will be reported later. Purposive works where the deliverable is the outcome table itself, which it usually is.

**Which passages within a source.** Long interviews and annual reports contain long stretches that cannot hold an outcome. Narrowing is legitimate. Record it as a step rather than leaving it an undocumented habit.

**Which statements to substantiate.** Covered on the [substantiation page](outcome-harvesting-substantiation.md#sampling-because-you-will-not-substantiate-everything). This is the sampling decision that most affects credibility. It is also the one most often made ad hoc.

Whichever you do, three conditions make a bound acceptable: somebody chose it on purpose, the number left out appears in the output rather than in a working note, and the cut falls on a boundary that means something for the question. A cut at "the first 30 documents" satisfies none of them.

## Working with programme staff as harvesters

Most harvests are drafted by programme staff with an evaluator facilitating. This is the standard arrangement, and it has one strength and one weakness, both large.

**The strength.** Staff know about changes that appear in no document and that no outsider would think to ask about. Second-order outcomes in particular surface almost entirely through people who were there.

**The weakness.** Staff cannot see their own outputs as outputs. The first draft from any programme team is an activity list with a few outcomes buried in it. Every time. Being told the definition does not fix it.

What works:

- **Teach with a rewrite.** Show one weak statement and its usable version, taken from their own programme, and let them see the diagnosis. The [worked rewrites](outcome-harvesting-identifying.md#worked-rewrites) are for this. A definition read aloud changes nothing at all.
- **Give them the three-part test as a checklist.** Named actor. Active past-tense verb. Date. They can apply that much to their own drafts without help.
- **Run the first round as a facilitated workshop.** Remote drafting against a template produces activity lists, because nobody is there to push back in the moment. A day in a room, drafting and correcting live, is worth three weeks of email.
- **Have them draft. Do not have them substantiate.** The evaluator owns substantiation, and staff must not approach substantiators about their own outcomes.
- **Say at the start that contribution claims are recorded as claims.** Doing this on day one makes it a method, and doing it at draft-report stage makes it feel like distrust. Same words, different reception.
- **Ask them for the failures directly and repeatedly.** Make it safe. Name it as the thing you most need from them, and show that a harvested negative is a finding rather than a mark against anybody.
- **Expect to edit heavily and say so in advance.** The harvester rewrites; the staff member corrects the rewrite. That loop is the working arrangement. Agreeing it before the first draft avoids an argument about ownership later.

## The coverage paragraph

Every harvest report needs one, in the methods section, along these lines:

> We read [n] documents covering [period], comprising [types]. We interviewed [n] people: [n] programme staff, [n] direct partners, [n] actors outside the direct partner set. [n] people on the informant list did not respond after two approaches. Drafting produced [n] candidate statements, reduced to [n] outcomes after deduplication and after removing [n] that described programme activity rather than a change in another actor. We substantiated [n] of these with [n] independent sources, selected on the following basis: [rule]. [n] statements remain unsubstantiated and are excluded from the counts in this report. We did not search [X] and did not approach [Y].

A commissioner can read that and know what they are holding. Most harvest reports contain no such paragraph. Their outcome tables are correspondingly hard to weigh.

## As a plan

The distinction between a search and a sample decides which record a coverage paragraph is written from. A sample step's output is the document list it drew, with its method and, for a drawn sample, its seed; where the document set is defined rather than drawn, the step takes the fixed list itself (`documents`), as agreed. A code step records what it actually read, including any document or section it could not, so between the sample step and each code step's record a coverage paragraph has its raw material: which documents, by what method, and how many were left unread, stated rather than reconstructed afterwards.

The five biases have to be written into the coding prompt rather than left for a coder to guard against, because the coder sees only the prompt and the column definitions. Telling the coder explicitly to look for second and third order actors and for the negative and disappointing half belongs in the prompt. Where the corpus spans a period and recency is a risk, the sample step's `stratify_by` can draw evenly across it only where the document list already carries a period or date as an attribute; where it does not, the document list has to be organised that way first.

The stopping rule, a defined document set fully read, a defined informant list fully covered, or a stated budget, is a fact about how the document list and the question were agreed before a workflow is designed. No piece reports against a stopping rule; the write step can be told to state what was read and what was not, drawing on the sample step's list and each code step's record, but the rule and whether it was kept is something the evaluator states.

Knowing whether the search was any good stays outside the workflow. The sample and code steps say what was read; nothing records who was invited and never replied, because no piece does outreach. The evaluator supplies the coverage paragraph's account of who was asked, and the answer never infers it from a document nobody flagged as missing.

Working with programme staff as harvesters sits entirely before any workflow runs, since drafting with staff is a facilitated human process; a workflow reads whatever that process has produced, as documents like any others. Where a run drafts candidate statements itself, it can stop once a few documents are coded so staff can correct the candidates before the rest are read, and its coverage paragraph is a draft until the evaluator has added who was asked.
