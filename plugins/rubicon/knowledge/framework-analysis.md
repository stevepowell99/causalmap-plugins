# Framework analysis

The matrix method of applied policy research: cases as rows, an agreed set of themes as columns, and in each cell what that case said on that theme, with a pointer back to the transcript. It is what most evaluators mean when they say they will chart their interviews, and in Rubicon it is one coding of every case against a fixed list of themes.

Read this when somebody names the method, asks for a framework matrix, asks to chart their data, or describes the thing without naming it: a grid of interviews against topics, filled in so the team can read across a case and down a theme. How a workflow does it is under [As a plan](#as-a-plan); the rest of this page is about the method, what it licenses and where it fails.

## What it establishes

What each case said on each theme, and therefore how cases compare. Reading across a row gives one case on every theme; reading down a column gives one theme across every case.

It licenses comparison within and between cases, and the description of a theme's forms and variety. It licenses nothing about how common anything is outside the cases read, because the sample was chosen to capture variety rather than to represent anybody. An evaluator who wants a prevalence has asked a question this method cannot answer, however full the matrix looks.

Two further things follow from the matrix being complete rather than from its contents. An empty cell is a finding about the material, and so is a case that sits against every other one.

## When it fits

- Semi-structured interviews covering the same topics with each respondent, which is the ordinary evaluation corpus. Gale et al. put it plainly: the method "is most commonly used for the thematic analysis of semi-structured interview transcripts", and "is most suitable for analysis of interview data, where it is desirable to generate themes by making comparisons within and between cases".
- Questions that arrive as a list: terms of reference, an interview guide's topics, the criteria of a rubric, the links of a theory of change. The list is already the columns.
- Work shared among several people, which is what the method was built for. The matrix is where a team's readings meet, and any member can see what any other put in a cell.
- Corpora where case attributes matter: role, site, sex, round. They sit beside the themes as columns of the documents, so subgroup comparison costs nothing extra.
- Deductive and inductive work alike. The framework can come from the terms of reference, from the material, or from both, which is why it suits evaluation better than methods committed to one or the other.

## When it does not

Say so before drafting anything, and offer what does fit.

- **Material on different subjects.** Gale et al.: "the Framework Method cannot accommodate highly heterogeneous data, i.e. data must cover similar topics or key issues so that it is possible to categorize it." A mixed bag of board minutes, field notes and two interviews cannot be given one set of columns, so the cells come out mostly silences that mean nothing. Read such a corpus for instances of the thing asked about instead, and count passages rather than cases.
- **Questions about how something was said or how it unfolded.** Gale et al. name three: "life history data might be better analysed using narrative analysis; recorded consultations ... using conversation analysis; and documentary data ... using discourse analysis". A cell holds what a case said on a theme, and none of those questions is about that.
- **Questions about the relations between themes.** A matrix puts each theme in its own cell, so what one theme does to another is left to whatever prose somebody writes underneath. Where the question is causal, code the links rather than the themes: a theory of change tested link by link, or Causal Map.
- **Prevalence in a population.** Never from a matrix, and never from any other reading Rubicon does.
- **A rare thing in a corpus too large to read whole.** Every case has to be read against every item, so a matrix over a thousand documents is priced accordingly. Screen first, and say what was not read.

## What data it needs

Documents that speak to the same topics, enough of them to compare, and a case list somebody can defend. Ten to forty interviews is the usual range; below about six, read the cases as cases and do not build a grid whose columns will each hold two entries.

The themes must be agreed before the reading that counts, whether the evaluator brings them or a discovery pass proposes them. A theme invented while reading case seventeen leaves the first sixteen unread against it, which is the commonest way a framework matrix comes out wrong.

## The stages

Gale et al. set out seven, and name each one:

1. Transcription.
2. Familiarisation with the interview.
3. Coding.
4. Developing a working analytical framework.
5. Applying the analytical framework.
6. Charting data into the framework matrix.
7. Interpreting the data.

The first two are the evaluator's. Coding and developing the framework are an open coding and its grouping, where the evaluator has no list already. Applying the framework and charting are one closed coding of every case against the list. Interpreting is a count and a write-up or a judgement, and it is the stage the method's own literature says gets skipped.

## What the matrix is for once it is filled

Gale et al. describe the work the filled matrix supports: "characteristics of and differences between the data are identified, perhaps generating typologies, interrogating theoretical concepts ... or mapping connections between categories to explore relationships and/or causality", so that the findings "can go beyond description of particular cases to explanation of, for example, reasons for the emergence of a phenomena, predicting how an organisation or other social actor is likely to instigate or respond to a situation, or identifying areas that are not functioning well". They also note what the display itself does: it facilitates "recognition of patterns in the data by any member of the research team, including through drawing attention to contradictory data, deviant cases or empty cells".

Of those, a workflow counts and compares across the matrix, reports the empty cells and the cases nothing is known about, and writes up or judges over the result. A typology of cases or a rating of each case is a second-order reading over its cells: where one is wanted, define it as a column read from each whole document (a `per_document` code step with a nominal or ordinal column), and say in the answer that it is a reading of the case rather than a count of its cells; do not improvise one in the write-up.

## Traps

- **Charting replaces the quotation with a summary.** The stage the method is named for is the stage where a person writes a phrase into a cell and the transcript recedes. That summary is an interpretive layer nobody recorded, and it is where an automated version would do most damage, since prose two steps from the source reads just as well whether or not anything supports it. A Rubicon row keeps the verbatim quotation, checked against its document, and the write-up illustrates only from the rows behind a count.
- **Counting the cells as a prevalence.** Gale et al.: "the 'spreadsheet' look perhaps further increases the temptation for those without an in-depth understanding of qualitative research to attempt to quantify qualitative data (e.g. '13 out of 20 participants said X). This kind of statement is clearly meaningless because the sampling in qualitative research is not designed to be representative of a wider population, but purposive to capture diversity around a phenomenon." A count over a matrix is a count within a named sample, with its base and its unit stated, and is never reported as a rate in a population.
- **Reading an empty cell as a no.** A case can be silent because it was never asked, because the interview went elsewhere, or because the theme does not apply to it. Only a case read whole with the item in front of the reader supports a zero, and a case never asked about the theme is not a case that said no.
- **A theme whose definition is loose.** Each cell is decided by the item's definition, so a single-word column such as "partnership" collects whatever anybody said near the word. Write each item as a codebook entry before the reading, with what counts and the near misses that do not.
- **Description in place of interpretation.** The matrix pulls towards restating what is in the cells theme by theme. The interpretation is the comparison: which cases differ, which cells are empty, and what the contradictory ones do to the finding.
- **Cases that are not one document each.** A person interviewed twice, a site with six informants and a focus group with eight speakers are each one row in the method and several documents in Rubicon. Say which the count is over.

## What good looks like

A reader can see the whole grid, not only the write-up. Every cell leads to a quotation they can look up. The themes were fixed before the reading, and the record says where they came from. Silences are visible and distinguished from material nobody read. Counts state their base and their unit, in words. The write-up says which cases disagree with the pattern, not only how many support it.

## As a plan

This is the matrix-of-cases pattern by another name: a fixed list of themes as the values of a nominal column, cases as documents, and a tabulate step over that column as the matrix, so there is no separate charting step to lose the quotation the way a hand-charted matrix does.

Where the evaluator has not agreed a list, an open code step with a free-text column reads a sample of documents for themes in the speakers' own words, and those labels are grouped into a short list of themes, each defined with what counts and the near misses, piloted on a couple of documents before it is trusted; where they have one already, from the terms of reference, an interview guide or a rubric's criteria, that list is written straight into the nominal column's values and the open pass is skipped. Either way the list is fixed before the reading that counts, because a theme invented while reading document seventeen leaves the first sixteen unread against it, which is the commonest way a framework matrix goes wrong. The method agrees its themes as a team and interprets the filled matrix together (stage seven), and a workflow cannot stand in for either: a grouped list is a draft for the evaluator and their team to agree, so a Rubicon run can stop once the definitions are written and a few documents coded, for them to agree what each theme means before everything is counted, and again once the answer is drafted, which is a draft to interpret with them before it is final.

A code step reads each document whole, once, against the whole theme column, writing a row wherever a case speaks to a theme from its own experience. A theme that gets no row for a document is not raised there, which a tabulate step by the theme column and by document shows as a cell of nought among the documents read, distinct from a document the plan never sampled, which never enters the base at all. A speaker's explicit "I do not know" is a third state the method wants and a theme column alone cannot carry: give it a column of its own on the same row, defined as tightly as the themes, so that a don't-know is counted apart from both a theme raised and a theme never raised.

A tabulate step by the theme column and by document, counting documents, gives who raised each item and how many, split by any document attribute the corpus carries, role, site, round, at no extra reading, always stated as a count within the documents read and never as a share of a population, since the sample was drawn for variety rather than to represent anyone.

Each theme's definition is written tightly enough, with what counts and the near misses that go elsewhere, that another careful coder would put the same passage under the same theme; where a placement is close, the row says so. The answer compares cases and reports contradictions rather than only restating what each theme contains.

Running the workflow against three or four contrasting documents first is where a theme worded loosely enough to collect whatever mentions a keyword gets caught, before the whole corpus is read against it.

## Relation to the wider matrix literature

Framework analysis is one instance of a larger family. Miles, Huberman and Saldaña treat a matrix as "essentially the 'intersection' of two lists, set up as rows and columns", and catalogue displays whose rows and columns are set differently: role-ordered, time-ordered, case-ordered, effects. Their p. 115 list of what a row or a column can be is a useful prompt when the rows are not obviously the documents: individuals, roles, relationships and groups, settings within sites, sites as wholes, specific actions, events, activities, strategies, meanings and perspectives, attitudes, values and beliefs, emotions and states, and processes.

They also name the moves that fill such a display. For drawing first conclusions: "noting patterns, themes; making contrasts, comparisons; clustering; and counting". For checking what comes out: "following up surprises, triangulating, making if-then tests, and checking out rival explanations". The first four are what a matrix and its counts do between them. The second four are the reason a plan does not stop at the count.

Where the question is about sequence or about what led to what, the same authors reach for a network rather than a matrix, since a network "lend[s] itself well to a case-oriented approach that re-creates the 'plot' of events over time". In Rubicon that is a theory of change tested link by link, or Causal Map's links.
