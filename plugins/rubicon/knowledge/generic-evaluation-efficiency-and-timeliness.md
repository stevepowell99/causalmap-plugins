# Generic evaluation questions: efficiency and timeliness

A plan for judging how quickly and economically an intervention turned resources into outputs: milestone slippage, procurement and administrative friction, and how fast management moved resources to unblock the work. It reads workplans, progress reports, reviews, partner interviews and procurement audits, and guards against full disbursement taken for delivery on time.

Back to [generic evaluation questions](generic-evaluation.md), for what the criterion pages share and for the works cited.

## Scope, and the contrast inside the corpus

Under the OECD-DAC guidance, efficiency asks how economically and how quickly resources, meaning money, expertise, labour and time, are converted into outputs and outcomes [1]. In a qualitative corpus an efficiency assessment turns on three dimensions [6].

- **Timeliness and schedule integrity.** Whether milestones were met as planned, where delivery was delayed, and what the delay did to the quality of the outputs [9].
- **Procedural and administrative friction.** The transaction costs of the organisation's own bureaucracy, complex procurement, heavy compliance overhead and long donor clearance cycles [6].
- **Allocative responsiveness.** How quickly and flexibly management moved money and people to clear bottlenecks or respond to changing conditions [3].

A frequent failure in qualitative efficiency reviews is treating disbursement as a proxy for speed [9]. A project can disburse its whole budget and still deliver major infrastructure two years late, too late to serve the season it was built for [9]. The contrast inside the corpus sets the milestone commitments in baseline workplans against the implementation timelines, procurement dates and accounts of friction in progress reports and field transcripts, on the column that says which phase each document belongs to [4].

## As a plan

A code step reads inception workplans against the later progress reports, split by partner, with one column recording a delay, its domain (procurement, staffing, approvals, and the like) and how critical it was to delivery, each a column of its own rather than inferred, and the passage's own account of who or what caused the delay kept as a column rather than assumed. A tabulate step gives the share of coded passages reporting a critical delay, by domain and by partner, always stated against the passages read rather than the whole portfolio, and a comparison between what the inception workplan promised and what the progress reports later recorded, since the contrast inside the corpus is what the criterion turns on.

The failure this page guards against, a full budget spend taken as proof of timely delivery, is guarded by writing the coding prompt to search for delay independently of any disbursement figure, so a well-spent budget cannot substitute for an absent search. Efficiency is a rule: a judge step holds the overall reading down wherever a partner's share of critical delays passes the stated threshold, whatever the disbursement rate shows, because spending cannot outweigh delays that leave outputs unused, applied by code rather than argued case by case.

The code step's `check` setting (off by default, and under test) has a second model reread every coded passage in its context against the coding instructions, keeping, recoding or dropping it with a reason. For it to catch a passage coded as reporting no delay where the later reports say otherwise, the coding instructions must say that the later reports are read as closely as the inception plan and that the workplan is never the default account of what happened. Since the write step states every count with the base it was taken from, a disaggregation by partner carries its own base, the number of reports read for that partner, by construction, so a thin base is visible rather than something a later check must catch.

A trial carries out the workflow on one partner's full set of reports before the rest, since the phasing of delay through a small corpus is where an ambiguous column definition, what counts as critical, gets caught cheaply.

## What it guards against

Evaluators routinely meet what might be called exogenous excuse bias, where an implementing agency puts every delay down to shocks in the wider context [9]. A column records, wherever a passage describes a delay or friction, whether the passage puts its cause in the organisation's own procedures, in a partner's operational capacity or in a disruption from outside [4]. Counts of delivery split by implementing partner show how partners differ: where several partners kept to schedule in the same volatile environment, a claim that the context made failure unavoidable is put in doubt [4]. Read worst-first, timely delivery of outputs cannot outweigh serious administrative and financial friction left unresolved [4].

## Where Rubicon falls short

- **The case is a document, never a milestone.** "Over 85% of milestones" can only be read as a share of the coded passages about milestones, and a milestone that five reports mention is five passages. There is no way yet to make the milestone the case, the same gap there is for a person whose accounts are spread across several documents.
- **Dates and money in a finance system are out of scope.** Planned against actual dates held in a tracking sheet, cost per output and disbursement numbers do not have any words behind them, and Rubicon refuses a question whose answer is in a spreadsheet; the answer covers the part the documents speak to, and those numbers are brought in when the report is written [4]. A delay or a cost stated in a report or an interview is in scope, since it has a speaker and a position in a source [4].
