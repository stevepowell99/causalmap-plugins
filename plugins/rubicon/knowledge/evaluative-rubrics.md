# Evaluative rubrics

How to write and apply an evaluative rubric, the criteria, levels, descriptors and synthesis rule that turn a description of what the documents show into a judgement of how good it is; read it whenever a question asks how well, to what extent, whether good enough, or for a rating, and before writing any judge step.

Related pages: [generic evaluation questions](generic-evaluation.md), whose criterion pages are worked rubrics for the OECD-DAC criteria; [evidence quality rubrics](evidence-quality-rubrics.md), for a rubric over the evidence behind one causal claim rather than over the programme; [QCA calibration](qca-calibration.md#calibration-is-rubric-work), where a fuzzy set is a rubric under another name.

## What it establishes

A rubric licenses a verdict of worth that a reader can trace and dispute: this programme was adequate on reach, because the evaluator's standard for adequate is X, and the evidence shows Y. Evaluation differs from description by that step from what is to how good it is, and Scriven's point is that the step needs premises of its own. The facts do not say where adequate begins. A rubric writes those premises down before the evidence is read, so the verdict follows from a standard somebody owns rather than from the analyst's impression.

What it does not establish: that the standard is the right one. A rubric makes the values explicit; it does not settle them. Two stakeholders with different rubrics can read the same evidence and reach different verdicts, and both can be correct on their own standard. That is a finding to report, not a fault to resolve.

## The parts

Scriven's logic of evaluation, as Fournier set it out, has four moves: establish criteria of merit, construct standards for each, measure performance against them, and synthesise into a judgement. A rubric is the second and fourth of those written in advance.

- **Criteria.** The dimensions of merit, each a question somebody could answer from evidence: did the programme reach the people it was meant for; were the services delivered of a quality participants valued. Usually from the terms of reference, the programme's own objectives, or the OECD-DAC criteria, and agreed rather than invented by the analyst.
- **Levels.** The named grades, best first: excellent, good, adequate, poor; or met, partly met, not met. Three to five is usual. A level name alone is not a standard.
- **Descriptors.** For each criterion and level, what the evidence has to show to earn it. "Most participants describe a change they can date and name a cause for, and no group the programme targeted is absent from those accounts" is a descriptor; "good reach" is not. The descriptors are where the work is, and the boundary between adequate and poor is the one that carries weight, so write it first.
- **The synthesis rule.** How the criteria's levels make an overall verdict, if one is wanted.
- **When no verdict can be given.** What the evidence would have to lack for a criterion to be unrateable: too few sources bearing on it, a question the documents cannot answer, a premise the material shows to be false.

Rubrics are analytic, a level for each criterion separately, or holistic, a single description of what excellent, good and poor look like across everything. Analytic rubrics are easier to apply consistently and to argue with; holistic ones read better and hide where the judgement turned. Use analytic rubrics for anything that will be checked.

## Synthesis

Never average ordinal levels. Good, poor and good do not make good-minus: they make a programme that failed somewhere, and the average hides where. The common rules, each of which has to be chosen and written down before the evidence is read:

- **Weakest decides.** The overall verdict is the lowest level any criterion reaches. Right for a chain, such as a theory of change whose outcome needs every link, and for any rubric where each criterion is a condition rather than a contribution.
- **Best decides.** Where criteria are alternative routes to the same end, either of which will do.
- **Bars, or hurdles.** Davidson's term for a criterion that caps the verdict whatever the others say: a programme that harmed the people it served cannot be rated good overall however efficient it was. A bar is a weakest-decides rule applied to one criterion only.
- **Rules in order.** The evaluator states which combinations give which verdict, tried in order with a final "otherwise": good where reach is at least adequate and quality is not poor; adequate where one of the two is adequate; otherwise poor.
- **No synthesis.** Report each criterion and say they were not combined. Often the honest choice, and the right one where the commissioner did not ask for an overall verdict, since an overall verdict is the line that gets quoted without its qualifications.

Weighting criteria numerically and summing (Davidson's "numerical weight and sum") is a fifth option in the literature, and the usual objection to it holds: numerical weights let large scores on minor criteria buy back failure on a major one.

## Who writes it, and when

King and colleagues argue that a rubric's value lies as much in the conversation that produces it as in the document: commissioner, programme staff and, where possible, the people the programme serves agreeing what good would look like surfaces values that would otherwise be argued about after the findings arrive. Two rules follow from their account and from Davidson's.

The rubric is agreed **before the evidence is read**. A standard chosen after seeing the numbers can be fitted to any of them, and nobody reading the report can tell. Revising a rubric during the work is legitimate when something unexpected shows the original was wrong; the revision is then recorded with its reason, and the verdict on the original rubric reported beside the new one.

The rubric belongs to **somebody named**. The analyst can draft one, propose levels, and point out where descriptors leave a gap, but a verdict on a standard nobody outside the analysis agreed is the analyst's opinion with a table attached. Where the evaluator has no rubric and does not want to write one, stop at the evidence table and say the verdict waits on their standard, offering two or three standards in their own terms, without numbers, for them to choose from.

## Rules and readings

Each criterion is judged one of two ways, and the rubric says which.

A **rule** turns on a count: at least two thirds of the partners interviewed describe the change; supporting accounts outnumber contradicting ones by two to one. A rule is applied by arithmetic on the counts, never by a reading of them, and it must test exactly what the standard says: the same quantity (people where the standard says people, a share where it says a share), over the base the standard names, at its threshold. A rule that tests something nearby is a standard of the analyst's own. A standard that promises a ratio needs both sides counted.

A **reading** weighs quoted evidence against descriptors that cannot be counted: whether the quality of training was what participants needed. The reader gives a level, cites the passages it relied on, names the evidence that pulls the other way, and says where the evidence is too thin to place. A reading is the right tool for a criterion of quality, and the wrong one for anything a count could settle.

A third kind rests on a number the documents state rather than one the coding counts: a monitoring report's figure for how many of the target households were reached. Quote it, with its base, and apply the threshold to it; do not recount it from narrative documents that were never meant to carry it.

## Traps

- **A standard supplied by the analyst.** The commonest fault, and invisible in the report, which reads the same either way. Test: can every threshold and descriptor be traced to the evaluator's words or a document they pointed to?
- **Thresholds set after the counts.** Test: was the rubric dated, or agreed in writing, before the reading?
- **A rule that tests something nearby.** "At least half of participants" applied as "at least half of the participants who mentioned it". Test: is the base in the rule the base in the standard?
- **Averaging levels**, or weighting and summing. Test: could the verdict be good with one criterion poor?
- **A verdict name that travels further than its description.** Where the weakest criterion decides, the overall verdict carries that criterion's level, so name verdicts for the level ("partly met") rather than for one criterion's failing.
- **Gaps between descriptors.** Evidence that falls between adequate and poor cannot be placed. Test: for each criterion, is there a level for every pattern of evidence, including none?
- **No level for "cannot say".** A rubric that forces a level on thin evidence turns absence of evidence into a poor rating. Test: does each criterion say when it cannot be rated?
- **A verdict without its table.** A verdict compresses the analysis into a word that tracing back does not rebuild. Test: does every verdict sit beside the counts, cases and quotations it read, and the level each criterion reached?
- **Criteria the documents cannot speak to**, such as ethics or transferability to another setting, scored from transcripts anyway.

## What good looks like

The rubric appears in full in the report, with whose it is and when it was agreed. Every criterion has levels with descriptors, a rule or a reading, and a statement of when it cannot be rated. The synthesis rule is written down and is not an average. Each verdict sits beside the evidence it read, with the evidence against it named. Where stakeholders' rubrics differ, the report gives each verdict on each rubric rather than choosing between them. A reader who disagrees with a verdict can see whether they disagree with the standard or with the reading of the evidence, which is the whole point.

## As a plan

A rubric becomes a judge step, after the tabulate steps whose counts its rules read and the code steps whose rows its readings weigh.

- **The standard first.** The judge step's `source` names whose standard it is, or the document it is quoted from. Every level and every combining rule says where it came from: `from: evaluator`, with the evaluator's own words quoted exactly, or `from: proposed`, where their standard did not settle it (a number they did not give, a descriptor that had to be written). Proposed levels go back to the evaluator before anything is read. Drafting is all a workflow can do here: the conversation in which commissioner, staff and the people served agree what good looks like cannot be stood in for by reading documents, so the workflow proposes criteria, levels and descriptors, and reads a rubric already agreed where the documents hold one. A Rubicon run can stop once the rubric and definitions are written and a few documents coded, for stakeholders to agree what each level means before everything is counted, and again once the verdict is drafted, which is a draft to make sense of with the people it concerns before it is final.
- **Verdicts** are the overall levels, best first, each with what it means. **Criteria** each have an id, the question, a kind (`rule`, `reading` or `stated`), and levels that leave no gap. A criterion rated on its own scale, such as good, adequate and poor, gives that scale; one rated on the overall verdicts does not need to.
- **A rule** names the tabulation it counts in, the cells it counts (`where`), the base the count is out of (`base` for a document attribute, `base_where` for a group the coding finds, such as those who took part), and levels as thresholds tried strongest first, the last with none.
- **A reading** names the rows it weighs and gives each level a descriptor.
- **A stated criterion** quotes the background document's words holding both numbers, with the number and its base.
- **Combine** is `weakest`, `best`, `separate`, or the evaluator's rules in order. Never an average.
- The code steps have to produce what the rules count. Design the coding backwards from the rubric: a criterion that asks about participants needs a column, or a document attribute, saying who is a participant; a ratio needs both sides coded.
- Where the evaluator gives no standard, leave the judge step out, deliver the tables, and say under `cannot_answer` that the verdict waits on their standard, offering two or three in their terms without numbers.

The write step reports each verdict beside its table: for each criterion, the counts or the passages, the threshold or descriptor, and the level reached; then the synthesis rule and the overall verdict, or a sentence saying none was asked for.
