# Causal loop diagrams

A causal loop diagram links variables, gives each link a plus or a minus, and looks for the closed loops the links make, marking each loop as reinforcing or balancing, so an evaluator can say why a change keeps growing, levels off or undoes itself; read it when a question is about whether results will last, why they stalled or spread, or what a programme set off that nobody planned.

Subsidiary page: [causal loop diagrams in Rubicon](causal-loop-diagrams-in-rubicon.md), which says how a workflow codes signed links, finds loops and draws them. Built on [causal mapping](causal-mapping.md), whose coding against a closed codebook, filters and traps still hold; this page covers what is different, which is the variables, the signs and the loops.

## What it establishes

The factors in a causal loop diagram are variables, each named so that it can go up or down: "Household income", "Trust in the clinic", "Staff workload". A signed link from C to E says that, in the source's account, a change in C moves E the same way (a plus link) or the opposite way (a minus link), other things equal. "More training meant more confidence" and "without the training, people lost confidence" are the same plus link from Training to Confidence; "the extra workload wore down morale" is a minus link from Staff workload to Morale. This is where a loop diagram parts company with a causal map, whose factors are propositions and which keeps "high leads to high" apart from "low leads to low"; a loop diagram does not. A loop is a chain of links that returns to where it started. Its polarity is the product of its signs: with no minus links, or an even number of them, a change anywhere in the loop comes back to push the same way, and the loop is **reinforcing** (R); with an odd number, the change comes back to push against itself, and the loop is **balancing** (B), sometimes called stabilising or goal-seeking.

Built from documents, a causal loop diagram establishes the same kind of claim as a causal map: that these sources describe a feedback loop, told whole within some sources' own narratives and linked up from the parts others know. It is evidence about the structure people believe they live in. It is not a model of the system, and the gap matters more here than for a map, because a loop invites a story about behaviour over time that nobody told.

What it cannot establish:

- **Which loop dominates.** Whether a reinforcing loop outruns the balancing loop that limits it depends on strengths and delays that narrative does not give. Richardson's critique of causal loop diagrams argues that a loop diagram does not show what the system will do over time. That takes a simulation model built and calibrated with stakeholders, which is system dynamics, not this.
- **How fast or how strong.** A thick arrow is still much said, not a strong effect. A delay can be recorded where a source states one, but its length is the source's estimate.
- **That a loop linked up from several sources operates as one loop anywhere.** This is often exactly how loops come to light, since one group of people sees one side of a loop and another group sees the other side. But each link was true for somebody, perhaps in a different place or time, so a loop nobody told whole is a hypothesis for the evaluator and the people involved to check.
- **A counterfactual.** As for any map.

## Answering evaluation questions with loops

Loops earn their place when the evaluation question is about change over time, and several of the standard criteria are. Each use below names the loop evidence that bears on the question and the standard an evaluator has to set before reading; the verdict itself comes from a rubric (see [evaluative rubrics](evaluative-rubrics.md)).

- **Sustainability: will the benefits last?** The evidence for is a reinforcing loop that runs through the outcome and does not need the programme's money to keep turning: higher income lets a household reinvest, which raises income again. The evidence against is either a balancing loop that erodes the gain (more producers enter, prices fall, income falls back), or a reinforcing loop whose engine is the funding itself, which will run backwards when it stops. A typical rubric: benefits are likely to last where at least a stated number of sources tell a reinforcing loop through the outcome with no funded link in it, and no balancing loop through the outcome is told by more sources. See [sustainability and exit](generic-evaluation-sustainability-and-exit.md).
- **Effectiveness: why did results level off or fall short?** A balancing loop that limits success is the usual answer people give: success brings a constraint (demand outruns trained staff, staff quality falls, demand falls). Reporting the limiting loop turns "results plateaued" into a finding about what to change. See [effectiveness](generic-evaluation-effectiveness.md).
- **Impact and unintended effects.** A loop through a factor no plan named is where unintended effects compound. The question is whether it is reinforcing, and so likely to grow. See [impact](generic-evaluation-impact.md).
- **Equity: who gains more over time?** A reinforcing loop of "success to the successful", where those who benefit first gain the means to benefit again, predicts widening gaps even where everybody gains. Compare the loop's sources by group. See [equity and differential reach](generic-evaluation-equity-and-differential-reach.md).
- **Theory of change testing.** Many theories of change assume a loop without drawing one: demonstration effects, peer learning, confidence building on success. Code the assumed loop's links as an agreed list and count, link by link, the sources telling each, as [contribution analysis](contribution-analysis.md) does for a chain. A loop is no stronger than its least-told link, the same rule as a chain.
- **Scaling and adaptation.** A reinforcing loop is where a small push goes furthest, and a balancing loop is where a push meets resistance. Say which links people describe as within the programme's reach.

The common systems archetypes (limits to success, fixes that fail, shifting the burden, success to the successful, escalation, tragedy of the commons) name recurring pairs of loops. Matching a corpus's loops to an archetype is useful as a prompt for what to look for next, and weak as a finding: say which links of the archetype the sources actually told.

## When it fits, and when it does not

It fits where the question concerns change over time (lasting, spreading, levelling off, backfiring), where sources describe consequences feeding back ("and because of that, more people came, which meant..."), or where different groups each see part of a cycle, and where a closed list of variables exists or can be agreed, since loops only close where the same variable is recognised at both ends.

It does not fit:

- **Where the question wants a forecast or a size.** "How much will income grow after the project ends" needs a model and data. Offer the loop evidence and say what a model would need.
- **Where open labels are never closed.** Every factor named once and in its own words makes no loops at all.
- **Where most links carry no sign.** "The drought affected the harvest" says nothing about which way. If most links are like that, loop polarity cannot be read.

## What data it needs

The same as causal mapping: text in which people explain causes, from more than one source. Accounts that follow consequences on over months or years carry more feedback than accounts of one episode. A group model building session, a workshop or a reflective interview asking "and then what happened?" gathers loops far better than a structured questionnaire.

## The steps in practice

**Variables.** Each factor is a variable named in its positive form, so that "more of" it makes sense, and never a direction or a state: "Household income" rather than "Income fell", "Morale" rather than "Low morale". A source's "less X" or "loss of X" is coded against X, and the direction is carried by the sign, not by the label.

**Signs.** Each link carries a plus or a minus as well as its two ends: plus where the two variables move together in the source's account, minus where they move opposite ways. "Prices fell, so fewer farmers sold" is a plus link from Price to Sales, since both moved down. A link whose direction the source does not give is marked unclear rather than guessed.

**Coding the way back.** Code a link from effect back to cause wherever the source states one, which a coder looking for forward chains tends to miss. Never add the return link because another source told it.

**A closed list of variables.** Either an agreed list, or labels in the respondents' words grouped into a short list of variables and the documents read again against it, as for a causal map with a codebook (the two closed routes on the [causal mapping in Rubicon](causal-mapping-in-rubicon.md) page).

**Finding loops.** Read each narrative for loops first: a coder looking for feedback within one account finds return links a coder following forward chains misses. Then combine every source's links into one map, as a causal map does, and find every elementary loop (one that visits no variable twice) up to a stated length, four or five links being sensible. For each loop, say how many sources tell all of it within their own narrative and how many sources tell each of its links. Both are evidence; they are different evidence.

**Polarity.** Count the minus links in each loop: even is reinforcing, odd is balancing. A loop with an unclear link has unknown polarity and is reported as such. Where sources disagree about a link's sign, both signs stay on the map, so the same cycle of variables can appear once as reinforcing and once as balancing, each with its own sources, which is itself a finding.

**Sensemaking.** Show the loops to the people who live in the system before anything is concluded from them, especially those nobody told whole. Group model building and participatory system dynamics treat this as the core of the method; a loop the participants do not recognise is a coding artefact or a finding worth a second look, and only they can say which.

## Drawing loops

A loop diagram draws each loop as a circle of factors, signs at the arrowheads, and the loop's R or B with a numbered marker (R1, B2) in its centre, with the direction of circulation. Links that sit in no loop belong on the diagram too: a causal loop diagram normally shows the variables that drive a loop from outside (a policy, a grant, the weather) and the outcomes it feeds, and in an evaluation the programme itself is usually one of these drivers, so the arrows from it into a loop are often the point. The whole structure, all loops on one map, is the hairball a causal map warns against, made worse because every arrow now carries a sign. Draw one diagram per loop, or per pair of loops that interact (a reinforcing loop and the balancing loop that limits it), and say beside each how many sources told it whole and how many told each link.

## Traps

- **Linked-up loops read as told.** The transitivity trap applies to loops as to paths: two sources may mean different things by the variable they share, or speak of contexts where the loop does not close. Say how many sources told each loop whole, beside each link's own count.
- **A direction hidden in a label.** "Reduced stress" as a variable turns a minus link into a plus one and silently reverses a loop's polarity. Every variable is named positively; check the list before reporting any polarity.
- **Guessed signs.** A sign the source did not state decides the loop's type as much as any stated one. Mark it unclear.
- **Reading behaviour off the diagram.** "This reinforcing loop will drive growth" is a forecast, and loops told in interviews say nothing about which one wins. Say that the sources describe the loop, and what would decide its effect.
- **Archetype fitting.** Seeing "limits to success" because the name fits, then reading the corpus for it. Say which links were told.
- **The circular layout everywhere.** Laying out a whole map in a circle makes everything look like one loop. Circles are for loops.
- **Counts read as prevalence.** As for maps, a source count is a floor on who tells a loop.

## What good looks like

- Every link on a loop traces to a quotation and a source, with its sign visible and justified by the words.
- Every variable is named positively, and the list of variables is stated.
- Each loop says how many sources told it whole and how many told each link.
- Polarity is stated for each loop and shown in the diagram, loops with an unclear link are named as unknown, and links whose sign sources dispute are shown both ways.
- The evaluative standard was set before the loops were counted, and the verdict follows from it.
- Nothing is claimed about which loop dominates, how fast anything grows or what would have happened otherwise.
- The report says where the people in the system have checked, or are still to check, the loops it found.

## As a plan

The workflow is a causal coding over a closed list of variables with a sign column, a tabulation of the loops the combined links make, and a judge step whose rules are written against loop counts. [Causal loop diagrams in Rubicon](causal-loop-diagrams-in-rubicon.md) gives it step by step.
