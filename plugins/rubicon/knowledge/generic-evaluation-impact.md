# Generic evaluation questions: impact

Read this when a terms of reference asks what difference a programme made, what its longer-term or higher-level effects were, intended or unintended, positive or negative, or whether it contributed to lasting change in systems, norms or people's wellbeing, and the evidence is documents holding accounts of change rather than a measured effect. It covers the OECD-DAC impact criterion, says which causal method to route the question to, and guards against a count of people crediting the programme read as the size of its effect.

Back to [generic evaluation questions](generic-evaluation.md), for what the criterion pages share and for the works cited.

## Scope, and the contrast inside the corpus

The 2019 definition asks "What difference does the intervention make?": the extent to which it has generated or is expected to generate "significant positive or negative, intended or unintended, higher-level effects". Its note speaks of "the ultimate significance and potentially transformative effects" of the intervention, longer term or broader in scope than those under effectiveness, including "holistic and enduring changes in systems or norms" (OECD DAC 2019, p. 11). The 2021 guidance names five elements (OECD 2021, pp. 65 to 66).

- **Higher-level effects**, beyond the results the programme was set up to deliver, which [effectiveness](generic-evaluation-effectiveness.md) covers.
- **Significance**: how much the change mattered to those involved, judged from several perspectives, keeping the evaluator's own value judgements out of it.
- **Differential impact**: who gained and who lost, since a positive overall picture can hide negative effects on some groups.
- **Unintended effects**, positive and negative, with particular attention to negative effects on vulnerable groups and the environment.
- **Transformational change**: enduring change in systems or norms, such as gender roles.

The guidance warns that "impact" is often confused with impact evaluation, meaning methods that establish a statistical causal relationship, and asks that its meaning be agreed at the outset (OECD 2021, p. 64); a terms of reference that says "impact" may want either. Documents do not contain a counterfactual. They contain accounts of change, often with the programme's part claimed by the speaker, so the answer is a set of accounts with their claimants: what changed, according to whom, what they credit, and what else they name. [Generative and counterfactual causation](generative-and-counterfactual.md) explains why such accounts are evidence about the counterfactual case by case, and never an estimate of its size.

The contrast inside the corpus is whose account a change comes from: the implementer's reports and case studies, the people the change is said to have happened to, and actors without any stake in the programme. A document attribute recording the speaker's role sets them side by side.

## As a plan

**Choose the method first.** Most impact questions have a method page.

- **Did the programme contribute to an observed change, given what else was happening?** A contribution question, which can be read as a direct link from the programme to each outcome, as a theory of change tested link by link, or as an open map of every causal claim with the paths from programme to outcome followed through it. [Contribution analysis](contribution-analysis.md) and [causal mapping](causal-mapping.md) set out those readings and what each needs.
- **Did a particular mechanism produce an important outcome?** [Process tracing](process-tracing.md), on the few outcomes the evaluation depends on.
- **What changed through other actors, including what nobody planned?** [Outcome harvesting](outcome-harvesting.md), mapped onto the pieces on [outcome harvesting in Rubicon](outcome-harvesting-in-rubicon.md).
- **For whom, and in what circumstances?** [Realist evaluation](realist-evaluation.md).
- **How much difference, on average?** None of these: it needs a comparison group and outcome data, commissioned outside the workflow.

Where the question asks for the programme's higher-level effects without naming a method, the general plan is this.

**Sample.** Every document, so that changes nobody planned can appear; stratify by the speaker's role only where the corpus is too large to read whole.

**Code.** One causal coding: the change described (the effect) and the cause the speaker names for it, one row per cause, the programme or anything else; and nominal columns for direction (positive, negative, mixed), for whether the speaker presents the change as one the programme aimed at, and, where documents mix voices, whose account it is. The coder sees only the prompt and the definitions, so the designer writes into the prompt what counts as a higher-level effect (change in people's lives, institutions, systems or norms, beyond an activity held or an output delivered); that every named cause is recorded, not only the programme; that negative and unplanned changes are looked for as carefully as positive ones; that a hoped-for change is not coded as one that happened; and that the coder records the claim without deciding whether it is true. "Intended" is coded against the results framework where the evaluator supplies it; otherwise the definition says it is the speaker's framing.

**Group.** Group the effect labels into kinds of change, define each kind, and record each row's kind as a nominal column; group the cause labels the same way, so the programme and its rivals are counted on the same footing. Keep changes in systems or norms apart from changes in individual circumstances when defining the kinds. These counts come from an open coding, so they are floors.

**Tabulate.** Count documents by kind of change and direction, and by kind of change against what is credited, so the `.within.` cells on the effect item say, of the documents describing a change, how many credit the programme and how many name something else. A tabulation by the speaker's role shows whose accounts each change comes from.

**Judge.** Significance and transformation are judgements of worth. Use a judge step only with the evaluator's standard, such as what counts as an enduring change in a norm and what evidence it needs. Without one, leave the judge out and offer standards in their terms without numbers: a change is significant where the people it affected describe it as mattering to them; a change is transformative where several actors outside the programme describe the same shift in practice or rules.

**Write.** The answer follows this page and [generative and counterfactual causation](generative-and-counterfactual.md) on how to state a causal claim. Each kind of change with who reports it and what they credit, rival causes beside the programme, negative and unplanned effects in full, and every impact claim written as an account attributed to its claimants ("seven of the twelve community interviews describe..., and five of them credit the programme"), never as a finding that the programme caused it.

**What the workflow must say it cannot answer** (its `cannot_answer`).

- **The size of the effect, or whether the change would have happened anyway, as an estimate.** That needs a comparison group or a counterfactual design on outcome data, which the evaluator commissions separately.
- **Whether a reported change happened**, unless somebody without any stake in it gives an account of it.
- **Effects that appear after the documents were written.** Higher-level change often takes longer than the programme, and evaluations are often due when it closes (OECD 2021, p. 67).
- **Prevalence beyond the documents.** A count is out of the documents read, never out of a population they were not chosen to represent.
- **A verdict on significance**, without the evaluator's standard.

## What it guards against

The commonest fault in impact findings from documents is what might be called the attribution slide: a change is described, the programme is mentioned nearby, and the report says the programme caused it. The coder records each named cause apart from the change, the grouping counts rival causes on the same footing as the programme, and the writer attributes every claim to its claimant. The second fault is a set of success stories read as the programme's whole effect. The guidance notes that unintended effects are often left out of monitoring frameworks and asks evaluators to look beyond the formal project documents (OECD 2021, p. 68), so the coding asks for negative and unplanned change, and a wholly positive set is reported as a finding about the documents.

## Where Rubicon falls short

- **The documents are mostly the programme's own.** Where most accounts come from implementer reports and case studies chosen to illustrate success, the counts describe those accounts; say so, and recommend interviews with people outside the programme.
- **A causal coding finds what speakers state.** A change described without any cause, or a cause implied but not said, is coded thinly or not at all.
- **Higher-level change is often spread across documents.** A shift in a norm may show only when several documents are set side by side, which a coder reading one document at a time does not do. The write step can bring them together, but the pattern does not appear in any count.
