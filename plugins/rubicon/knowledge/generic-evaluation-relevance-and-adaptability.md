# Generic evaluation questions: relevance and adaptability

A plan for judging whether an intervention addressed what its beneficiaries said they needed, and whether it changed course when the context changed. It reads needs assessments, project documents, beneficiary transcripts, context monitoring and annual reviews, and guards against relevance checked at inception and never again.

Back to [generic evaluation questions](generic-evaluation.md), for what the criterion pages share and for the works cited.

## Scope, and the contrast inside the corpus

The 2019 definition asks "Is the intervention doing the right things?": "The extent to which the intervention objectives and design respond to beneficiaries', global, country, and partner/institution needs, policies, and priorities, and continue to do so if circumstances change" (OECD DAC 2019, p. 7). Its note adds that relevance "requires analysing any changes in the context to assess the extent to which the intervention can be (or has been) adapted to remain relevant" (OECD DAC 2019, p. 7).

Adaptability is not a DAC criterion. It is asked for under that name often enough to get a page, and it rests on those two phrases in the relevance definition and its note, together with the efficiency note's "timeframe reasonably adjusted to the demands of the evolving context" (OECD DAC 2019, p. 10). Where a terms of reference asks how well an intervention learned and changed course as it went, rather than whether it stayed relevant, developmental evaluation is the fuller account of that question (Patton 2011). The 2021 guidance takes relevance from p. 38, with its table of challenges on p. 42 (OECD 2021), and discusses the time dimension of the criteria in general on p. 26.

Terms of reference organise the question around three lines of inquiry.

- **Congruence with beneficiary priorities.** How far the activities matched the priorities, basic needs and economic realities of the target population, and of its marginalised subgroups in particular. The 2019 revision added beneficiaries' "priorities" to the definition to stress "the importance of affected people (not just donors or governments) deciding what is most important/urgent" (OECD DAC 2019, p. 7, Box 2).
- **Strategic and policy cohesion.** Consistency with the partner government's sector strategies, national priorities and the donor's policy commitments.
- **Adaptability to the context.** How quickly the intervention noticed a change in its context, such as economic instability, conflict, a policy change or a public health emergency, and updated its theory of change and workplans.

The dominant failure in relevance evaluation is what might be called the static inception fallacy. An evaluation records that the project matched national strategies and baseline assessments at inception, and passes over the project staying rigid after a shock changed local priorities. The plan contrasts along two axes. The first sets what communities said they needed, from needs assessments and beneficiary transcripts, against what the project delivered, from project documents and annual reviews. The second traces adaptation from the early years of the project to the late ones, on an attribute giving each document's implementation year, or one naming the phase, with inception and post-shock monitoring among its values, where the project has one.

## As a plan

**Sample.** Every document; where the corpus is too large to read whole, stratify by year or phase, so the later material is read as fully as the design documents.

**Code.** A nominal column for whose priorities a passage concerns (the beneficiaries' own, the government's, a donor's thematic mandate); an ordinal column for how well delivery met them, each level defined; a binary column for whether the passage describes a change in context, and another for whether it describes the intervention changing course in response; and a column for when the events described happened, where the passage says, since a document's own year can be years after the events it reports. The coding prompt reads the later material as closely as the needs assessment, and says that a passage counts only for the period it describes: a row describing good fit at design says nothing about fit five years on. Define the levels tightly enough that another coder would place the same passage the same way; where the call is close, the answer says so.

**Tabulate.** Count documents by whose priorities and level of fit, and by year or phase, so congruence at design sits beside congruence later; and count documents describing a change in context against those describing a change of course, by year. Every count is out of the documents read, not a share of anybody outside the corpus.

**Judge.** Only with the evaluator's standard. Relevance is usually a `reading`, worst-first: the adaptation rows weighed against a descriptor for failing to adapt, holding the verdict down wherever delivery plainly failed to change course after a documented shock, whatever the design phase showed, because relevance at the start cannot excuse a context the delivery never caught up with. Without a standard, leave the judge out and offer standards in their terms without numbers.

**Write.** Fit at design and fit later as separate findings, never one carried forward; the year, if any, the design stopped tracking the context; and the overall reading named for what actually failed (delivery did not adapt) rather than for the criterion's own label, since a verdict named for the weakest criterion's failing is what a reader will quote, and a general verdict on relevance would misread it.

**What the workflow must say it cannot answer** (its `cannot_answer`).

- **What beneficiaries needed, beyond what the documents record them saying.**
- **How fast a response was**, where no passage says how long it took; comparing dates across two reports is the evaluator's reading, not a count.

Try the definitions on the needs assessment and one later report before coding the rest.

## What it guards against

The main weakness in evaluating relevance is what might be called donor-mandate substitution, where close alignment with a donor's strategic objectives is used to cover serious mismatches with what communities need. The column recording whose priorities a passage concerns, counted against level of fit, sets the two side by side, so a gap between alignment with the donor and satisfaction in the community is visible in the table. Read worst-first, an intervention that did not change course when circumstances made its activities obsolete cannot reach the top verdict, in line with the definition's "continue to do so if circumstances change" (OECD DAC 2019, p. 7).

## Where Rubicon falls short

- **A year attribute is about a document.** A count by year places every passage in the year its document carries, so a retrospective review written in the final year about a shock three years earlier counts under the final year. The column for when the events happened places it properly, where the passage says.
- **A slow response is only as good as the text.** It needs a passage that says how long the response took; a coder reading one document at a time cannot infer a delay that depends on comparing two dated reports against each other.
