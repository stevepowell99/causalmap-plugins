# Generic evaluation questions: relevance and adaptability

A plan for judging whether an intervention addressed what its beneficiaries said they needed, and whether it changed course when the context changed. It reads needs assessments, project documents, beneficiary transcripts, context monitoring and annual reviews, and guards against relevance checked at inception and never again.

Back to [generic evaluation questions](generic-evaluation.md), for what the criterion pages share and for the works cited.

## Scope, and the contrast inside the corpus

Under the 2019 OECD-DAC revision, relevance asks whether an intervention is doing the right things, and asks for it to be appraised over time rather than confirmed once at design [1]. Terms of reference organise it around three lines of inquiry [1].

- **Congruence with beneficiary priorities.** How far the activities matched the priorities, basic needs and economic realities of the target population, and of its marginalised subgroups in particular [1].
- **Strategic and policy cohesion.** Consistency with the partner government's sector strategies, national priorities and the donor's policy commitments [1].
- **Adaptability to the context.** How quickly the intervention noticed a change in its context, such as economic instability, conflict, a policy change or a public health emergency, and updated its theory of change and workplans [1].

The dominant failure in relevance evaluation is what might be called the static inception fallacy [9]. An evaluation records that the project matched national strategies and baseline assessments at inception, and passes over the project staying rigid after a shock changed local priorities [9]. The plan contrasts along two axes [4]. The first sets what communities said they needed, from needs assessments and beneficiary transcripts, against what the project delivered, from project documents and annual reviews, and a code step's columns record, for each passage, whose priorities it concerns and how well delivery met them. The second traces adaptation from the early years of the project to the late ones, on a column giving each document's implementation year; a column naming the phase, with inception and post-shock monitoring among its values, serves the same purpose where the project has one.

## As a plan

A code step reads the community needs assessments against what the logframe and the later delivery reports show was actually provided, with columns for fit or adaptation and a year attached wherever the passage supports one, since adaptation by year is what tells a design that was relevant once from one that stayed relevant as the context changed. A tabulate step gives the congruence between what beneficiaries said they needed and what was delivered, and adaptation events by year, against the passages or documents read rather than a share of anybody outside the corpus.

The failure this criterion guards against, a design praised as relevant at inception while delivery failed to change course after a shock, is guarded by writing the coding prompt to read the later material as closely as the needs assessment: a row describing good fit at design says nothing about fit five years on, and the write step's instructions should treat them as separate findings rather than one carried forward. Relevance is a reading, worst-first: its judge step has a strong model weigh the adaptation rows against a descriptor for failing to adapt, holding the reading down wherever delivery plainly failed to change course after a documented shock, whatever the design phase showed, because relevance at the start cannot excuse a context the delivery never caught up with.

Naming the verdict matters more here than on the other criterion pages, because a verdict named for the weakest criterion's failing is what the reader will quote; the write step's instructions should name the overall reading for what actually failed, delivery did not adapt, rather than for the criterion's own label, so the answer is not misread as a general verdict on relevance.

The code step's `check` setting (off by default, and under test) has a second model reread every coded passage in its context against the coding instructions, keeping, recoding or dropping it with a reason. For it to catch good congruence read as still current, the coding instructions must say that a passage counts only for the period it describes. The write step's instructions should say which year, if any, the design stopped tracking the context. A trial carries out the workflow on the needs assessment and one later report before the full corpus runs.

## What it guards against

The main weakness in evaluating relevance is what might be called donor-mandate substitution, where close alignment with a donor's strategic objectives is used to cover serious mismatches with what communities need [3]. The plan counters it with a column recording what each passage says the intervention was aligned with, the beneficiaries' own priorities or a donor's thematic mandate, and counts split by that field set the two side by side, so a gap between alignment with the donor and satisfaction in the community is visible in the table [4]. Read worst-first, an intervention that did not change course when circumstances made its activities obsolete cannot reach the top verdict, in line with the 2019 OECD-DAC call for relevance judged over time [1].

## Where Rubicon falls short

- **A year is a column on a document.** A count by year places every passage in the year its source carries, so a retrospective review written in the final year about a shock three years earlier counts under the final year. A column on each coded row for when the events described happened would place it properly.
- **A slow response is only as good as the text.** It needs a passage that says how long the response took, and a code step, which reads one document at a time, cannot infer a delay that depends on comparing two dated reports against each other.
