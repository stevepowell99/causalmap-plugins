# Most Significant Change: analysing a corpus of stories

Read this when a question names MSC or brings a set of change stories to analyse: MSC itself collects stories and has stakeholder panels choose the most significant, which is not analysis, so this page says what the method is and how to analyse the whole corpus of stories it leaves behind.

Related pages: [outcome harvesting](outcome-harvesting.md), the other approach that starts from changes rather than from objectives; [causal mapping](causal-mapping.md), for the causal claims stories make; [evaluative rubrics](evaluative-rubrics.md), since a selection panel's reasons are an unwritten rubric.

## What MSC is, and what it is not

MSC is a participatory process for collecting stories of change and choosing among them. Rick Davies developed it in the 1990s for monitoring a rural development programme in Bangladesh, and the guide he wrote with Jess Dart (Davies and Dart, 2005) is the standard account. Stories are collected in agreed domains of change; at each level of the organisation a panel reads them and selects the most significant, writing down why; the selections and reasons are fed back to those who told the stories. Over repeated rounds the programme learns, and argues about, what it is for.

So MSC is not an analysis method. Its product is a conversation about values, held by the people involved, and its one analytic act, selection, belongs to the panels. An analyst or a model choosing the most significant stories replaces their values with its own.

What people usually want from documents is something else: an analysis of the whole set of stories collected, not only the ones chosen. The guide allows for this as secondary analysis (its step 9), and it is often the most useful thing a set of stories can give. It is ordinary qualitative analysis of a story corpus, and the rest of this page is about doing it well.

## What analysing the whole corpus can establish

What kinds of change people chose to tell stories about, who told them, what they credit for each change, and how those patterns differ between groups of tellers, domains or rounds. Where selection records exist, what the panels chose against what was told, and the values their reasons show.

What it cannot establish: how common any change is among the people the programme reached, what the average experience was, or that the programme caused a change. Stories are told because they seemed worth telling, and under MSC they are collected in search of significance, so the set leans towards the vivid and, usually, the positive. A story credits whatever its teller credits; counted across stories, those credits are accounts, not attribution.

## When it fits, and when it does not

It fits a corpus of stories gathered with some consistency (the same prompt, or the same domains), with who told each one recorded, large enough that patterns across stories mean more than any one of them.

It does not fit a question about reach, prevalence or the size of an effect, or a summative verdict on whether the programme worked, unless other evidence carries that part. A corpus of selected stories only is an anthology: it can show what the panels valued, not what the stories as a whole contain.

## What data it needs

Every story, each with who told it and who recorded it, its domain and round where there are any, and the teller's own account of why it matters. Where selection happened, the selection records: which stories each panel chose at each level, and their reasons. The prompt the stories answered, since it shapes what was told.

## The steps in practice

1. **Read the prompt and the collection process.** What were people asked, by whom, and was a negative or open domain offered? This sets what absence in the corpus can mean.
2. **Code each story** for the change it describes, its direction, whom it happened to, and what the teller credits for it.
3. **Group the changes into kinds**, each defined, so stories describing the same change in different words count together.
4. **Compare** kinds of change across tellers, domains and rounds, and, where selection records exist, between stories told and stories chosen.
5. **Code the panels' reasons**, where recorded, into kinds of value.
6. **Report** patterns as patterns in what was told, with the absences named.

## Traps

- **Selected stories read as typical.** Test: does any sentence describe the chosen stories as what the programme generally achieved?
- **Counts of stories read as prevalence.** A kind of change with many stories is one people chose to tell stories about. Test: is every count stated as stories, out of the stories collected?
- **Only positive change.** Without a prompt or domain for negative change, the corpus will be almost entirely positive. Test: does the report say what the prompt allowed, and report negative or unexpected change where found?
- **Credit read as cause.** Test: is every claim about what produced a change attributed to its tellers?
- **The analyst doing the selecting.** Test: are any "most significant" stories in the report the panels' choices, with their reasons, rather than the analyst's?
- **One prolific teller or recorder.** Stories gathered by one field worker, or several from one person, are not independent. Test: is the count by teller or recorder checked?

## What good looks like

The report opens with what the stories show: the kinds of change told, each defined, with counts stated as stories; differences between groups of tellers with their bases; what was told against what was chosen, where the records allow, with the panels' reasons; and the absences. Nothing is claimed about prevalence or attribution beyond what the stories themselves say. How the stories were collected and selected, and that the analysis covers the whole corpus, are stated after the findings, with the limits.

## As a plan

**Sample.** Every story; a story is one document. The teller's role, domain, round and selection status (chosen at which level, or not) are document attributes where recorded.

**Code.** A `per_document` code step: the change described (free text), its direction (better, worse, mixed), whom it happened to, and what the teller credits for it. Group the change labels into kinds, each defined, recorded as a nominal column; do the same for what is credited where the question asks why. Where selection records with reasons are among the documents, a second code step reads the reasons, a row per reason, grouped into kinds of value (lasting change, reaching the poorest, change in relationships, and so on).

**Tabulate.** Stories by kind of change and direction; by kind of change and teller, domain or round; by kind of change and selection status, each out of its own base; and the reasons by kind of value and selection level.

**Judge.** Rarely. A verdict on the programme needs the evaluator's rubric and evidence beyond the stories; the stories alone bear on what people valued and reported.

**Write.** What kinds of change were told, by whom, and what they credit; how this differs between groups; what was chosen against what was told, and the values the reasons show; what is absent, read against the prompt. Counts are of stories, never of people affected.

**What stays with people.** The analysis cannot stand in for the panels' selection, for feeding results back to the tellers, or for the programme's discussion of what it values; it reads their records where these are among the documents, and its kinds of change and of value are candidates for the people involved to correct. A Rubicon run can stop once those kinds are defined and a few stories coded, so the programme can agree what each kind means before everything is counted, and once the answer is drafted, which is a draft to discuss with them, and with the tellers where feedback is part of the round, before it is final.

**What the workflow must say it cannot answer.** Which stories are most significant, which is the panels' judgement; how common any change is; whether the programme caused it; and whether a story is accurate, unless verification (the guide's step 7) is in the documents.
