# Generative and counterfactual causation

Two accounts of what it means for a programme to have caused an outcome, which evaluation questions often assume without saying which, and what to propose when a question assumes one and the material suits the other.

Ask for this page when a question is about impact, attribution, contribution or additionality, when somebody asks whether a change would have happened anyway, when a user objects that there is no comparison group, and before proposing a coding whose rows will be read as evidence that something worked. How many passages such a question needs, and proportions of what, are on [reading enough](reading-enough.md).

## The two accounts

**Counterfactual.** X caused Y if Y would not have happened, or not in the same way, had X not happened. Lewis gave the philosophical version in 1973. In evaluation it becomes the difference between what happened and an estimate of what would have happened without the programme, usually taken from a comparison group, a baseline or a site that got nothing. What it naturally produces is a quantity: an effect, an average difference, a share of the outcome.

**Generative.** X caused Y if a process runs from one to the other: something X provided was taken up by somebody, it did something, and Y followed from that. The evidence is an account of the process in a case, so a causal claim can be established one case at a time, without a comparison. Realist evaluation, process tracing and contribution analysis all rest on it (see [realist evaluation](realist-evaluation.md) and [contribution analysis](contribution-analysis.md)). Stern and colleagues' report for DFID names it as one of four bases for causal inference, beside regularity, counterfactuals and multiple causation, and puts its limit plainly: "Generative causation is strong on explanation but weak on estimating quantities or extent of impact."

**Causal mapping is generative, and treats people as causality detectors.** When a respondent says the loan let her buy seed and the harvest paid the school fees, she is reporting a process in her own life from the place best suited to see it. Scriven holds that causation can be directly observed, "in lab or home or field". An observer can be wrong, as with any observation. A link in a causal map means only that somebody claims X somehow influenced Y. A map built from such links is a store of evidence for causal pathways rather than a model of the pathways themselves or of anybody's beliefs about them (Powell, Copestake and Remnant 2024). A Rubicon coding of causal claims is the same kind of thing: the speaker declares the cause, and the row keeps the declaration, who made it and where.

## How they relate

They are not rivals. A generative claim carries a counterfactual inside it. "The loan let me buy seed" says that without the loan she would not have bought it, or not then, so each declaration is evidence about the counterfactual question in one case, from one person placed to judge it. A respondent can also be asked the counterfactual outright: would this have happened anyway? Her answer is a judgement rather than a measurement, and it is still evidence.

What a set of declarations does not give is a quantity for a population. Nineteen households crediting the programme is not an estimate of how much the programme raised wellbeing on average. Households nobody spoke to are not in it at all.

The traffic also runs the other way. A counterfactual estimate says that a difference exists and how large it is. It says nothing about how the difference came about or why it varies from one place to another, which is the question realist evaluation exists to answer.

There is one case where a comparison can mislead and an account of the process cannot. Where two causes were each enough to produce the outcome, the outcome would have happened without either of them, so a difference-making test says neither mattered. Scriven makes this objection to the counterfactual definition, citing overdetermination, and the report by Stern and colleagues treats it, under pre-emption, as a problem the regularity and counterfactual approaches cannot address. An account of which cause actually did the work in this case answers it.

## When each fits

The counterfactual framing fits where the question is how much, where a comparison exists in the material or beside it, and where the commissioner will use a size. Rubicon cannot estimate a size of impact from text. Where the number lives in a dataset rather than in anybody's words, that part of the question is outside what it answers. What Rubicon can do from text, towards a counterfactual question, is three things:

- **Code each speaker's own counterfactual judgement**, where they give one, as a column.
- **Search for the rival explanations as hard as for the programme**, so that "it would have happened anyway" and "somebody else did it" are findings rather than assumptions. See [contribution analysis](contribution-analysis.md).
- **Compare documents across a column the sources already carry**, such as sites that got support against sites that did not. It holds only where the sites are alike in the ways that matter.

The generative framing fits where the question is how or why, where there are few cases or one, where the material is people explaining what caused what, where the programme works through other actors, and where the outcome differs between places and somebody wants to know why.

## What to propose

Some commissioners expect counterfactual language and would think little of a tool that could not speak it. Others work in a generative tradition and would think little of a tool that could speak nothing else. Rubicon needs both. Usually it acts on the distinction without naming it.

- **By default, code what the material declares.** A link from what a speaker names as the cause to what they name as the effect, with who said it. That is what the material contains.
- **A counterfactual question over narrative material is not refused, and not met with "there is no counterfactual here" and nothing else.** Propose what the material can answer that bears on it: what speakers credit for the change, the programme and every rival, found by searching on the outcome so every cause competes on the same ground; their own judgement of what would have happened otherwise, where they give one; and any comparison column the sources carry. Keep the commissioner's words, attribution, contribution, additionality, and say in one sentence what the answer will be: evidence about the counterfactual, case by case, and not an estimate of its size.
- **Where the question asks for a size**, say which part the plan answers and which needs data from outside the corpus.
- **Where the question is generative and the evidence is a comparison**, such as counts across sites, count the comparison, which costs no reading where the sources already carry the columns, and say that a difference between groups is what needs explaining rather than the explanation. The explaining needs passages. Nothing in Rubicon tests whether a gap between two small counts would survive, so report both counts with their bases and do not call the gap a difference.
- **Where the question presupposes the effect**, "how did the programme improve wellbeing?", code the outcome in both directions and every cause named, because the material may show that it did not, and a judgement may refuse the question on that ground.

**Name the distinction only where it changes what you propose.** That is when somebody asks for an effect size, when somebody asks whether a change would have happened anyway, and when a number such as "twelve of nineteen credited the programme" is about to be read as "the programme worked for 63%". Otherwise write the plan and say what its answer will be evidence of. A methodology lecture is not what a person asking a question came for.

## Traps

- **Reading a count of attributions as an effect.** "Twelve of nineteen credited the programme" is a fact about what nineteen people said. It may be the best evidence available and it is still not a share of a population helped.
- **Searching on the programme instead of the outcome.** The passages that come back all mention the programme, so the rivals never appear and the comparison was settled before any reading began.
- **Taking a vivid declaration as settled.** It is evidence from somebody placed to see a process, weighed by who said it, whether they saw it themselves, whether they were prompted, and what they stand to gain. The speaker columns on the [contribution analysis](contribution-analysis.md) page are the instrument.
- **Coding only improvement.** A programme credited with a gain in one household and a loss in another is two findings, and a coding that only looks for gains cannot find the second.
- **Objecting that there is no counterfactual as though that ended the matter.** It is true of most qualitative material and it is where the work starts.

## As a plan

What changed, who or what gets credit, and the speaker's own counterfactual judgement where they give one, are each a column of a code step rather than a separate record, and this page's advice applies alike to a code step's prompt, to grouping what it finds and to writing the answer: searching for attribution, rereading for the rival's own evidence, or proposing what to search for at all.

A code step is briefed on the outcome rather than on the programme, so every cause, the programme and every rival alike, competes for the same passages instead of the rivals being invisible from the start. Its central column records what the speaker credits, nominal, with the programme and every named rival as its own value rather than folded into a general "other", so a rival can be counted on its own once it recurs; direction and the speaker's counterfactual judgement can be columns of their own in the same step, each defined as tightly as the credit column, or, where the answer will read them from the quote rather than count them, kept inside the credit column's own value definitions.

A tabulate step by the credit column counts documents crediting the programme and documents naming a rival, each against the number of documents read, never against a population the corpus was not built to represent; its `.within.` cells give, for the documents whose outcome improved, how many also credited the programme, stated out of that narrower base rather than out of every document read. Where the question wants a size rather than a case-by-case account, the designer says so under `cannot_answer`, naming which part the workflow answers, an account of process, case by case, and which needs a comparison the material does not contain. Where a comparison column already sits on the documents, the tabulate step groups counts by it, and the write step states the two counts with their bases without calling a gap between them a finding, since nothing in the record tests whether it would survive a document added or removed.

The commonest misreading is a row read as crediting the programme where the speaker in fact names another cause. The credit column's values say what counts and the near misses that go to a rival, tightly enough that another careful coder would place the passage the same way, and a close placement is said so in its row. The answer names the distinction between the two accounts only where it changes what is claimed, a size where a case-by-case account was given, or the reverse, rather than as an aside nobody asked for.

Running the workflow on a handful of documents for both credits and rivals before the rest is where a code step worded around the programme rather than the outcome is a fault cheap to catch early and dear to catch once the whole corpus has been read.
