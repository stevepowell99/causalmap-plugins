# Identifying outcome statements

Steps 2 and 3 of an outcome harvest: what counts as an outcome statement and what does not, drafting from documents and interviews, and worked rewrites of weak drafts. Drafting is where the whole exercise is won or lost, because nothing downstream can repair a set of statements that are too vague to substantiate.

Back to [outcome harvesting](outcome-harvesting.md).

## The test

**A named social actor did something differently, at a stated time and place, and somebody other than the reporter could confirm it.**

Four things have to be present. An actor you can name. An act, in the past. A date. A source that could verify it. If any one is absent the statement is not yet usable, whatever else it says.

The working definition of an outcome in this method is a change in the behaviour, relationships, actions, activities, policies or practices of an individual, group, community, organisation or institution. The operative word throughout is behaviour. A change in what somebody thinks, knows, feels or is capable of counts only when it has surfaced as something they did.

## What counts

- **A formal act.** A policy adopted, a law passed, a budget line created, a guideline issued, a committee established, a contract signed, a document published containing a specific provision.
- **A practice change.** A team using a new form, a new step in a procedure, a meeting series that keeps happening, a changed selection rule, a reallocated post.
- **A relationship change.** Two organisations that now work together, an actor admitted to a forum they were previously outside, a referral route that now operates.
- **A resource decision.** Money moved, staff assigned, equipment procured, a service extended to a new area.
- **A stopping or a reversal.** A ministry withdrawing a draft bill, a donor dropping a condition, a partner ending a harmful practice. Negative-sounding acts are outcomes; so are positive-sounding acts that turn out badly.
- **A change in what somebody says publicly**, where the saying is itself the act: a minister's public commitment in a speech, a position taken in a written submission, a published editorial line.

## What does not count

- **Programme outputs.** A training delivered, a report published by the programme, a workshop held, a toolkit produced, a grant disbursed. These record what the programme did. Staff supply these constantly and will keep supplying them after being told twice.
- **Knowledge, awareness, attitude and capacity claims.** "Staff are more aware of gender issues." "The coalition's capacity was strengthened." These are internal states inferred by the reporter. They become outcomes only when expressed as an act.
- **Intentions, plans and commitments to act later.** "The Ministry committed to reviewing the policy." A commitment counts only where the commitment itself is an observable, dated act with a record (see the worked example below).
- **Judgements about the programme.** "The project was well received." "Partners valued the technical support." That is satisfaction data. It belongs somewhere else.
- **Abstractions with no actor.** "Coordination improved." "The enabling environment strengthened." Nobody can confirm these and nobody can date them.
- **Aggregate counts as a single statement.** "Forty CSOs improved their governance." That is either forty statements or one unverifiable claim. Usually the latter.
- **Changes in the programme's own behaviour**, unless the harvest design put them in scope in step 1. Internal learning outcomes can be legitimate. Mixing them into the same set without a flag makes the actor counts meaningless.

## Edge cases worth settling in advance

- **The absence of an expected change is not an outcome.** It may be an important finding. It also has nowhere to live in an outcome set. Record non-occurrence separately, in the analysis notes, or it will be lost. This matters more than it sounds: a set that contains only occurrences and an analysis that never mentions non-occurrence reads as a much better programme than the evidence supports.
- **One-off versus established.** A single instance of new behaviour is weaker evidence than a practice sustained over several cycles. Carry it as a field (`first_instance`, `repeated`, `institutionalised`) rather than arguing case by case. Then the analysis can use it.
- **Where the actor sits.** A direct partner (first order), somebody the partner influenced (second order), somebody beyond that (third order). Record the order at drafting. It is close to impossible to reconstruct later and it is one of the more informative fields.
- **Anticipated but unplanned.** Intended and unintended are worth separating from positive and negative, because the four combinations behave differently in analysis. An unintended positive is usually the most valuable thing in a harvest.
- **Collective acts.** Where three ministries jointly signed one memorandum, that is one act by three actors. Where three ministries each adopted the same tool separately, that is three outcomes. The test is whether the acts are separately verifiable and separately contributed to.

## Drafting from documents

1. **Define the document set first and write it down.** Which reports, which period, which partners. This becomes the coverage statement in the report and, if you want any percentage later, the frame.
2. **Draft as you read, one statement per candidate change, with the source reference attached at that moment.** Retrofitting sources after a reading marathon is where harvests come apart. You will not remember which of four similar quarterly reports carried the sentence.
3. **Capture the programme's own words verbatim first, then rewrite.** Rewriting straight from memory loses the specifics that make substantiation possible.
4. **Expect a low yield.** A sixty-page annual report giving three usable outcomes is normal. Most programme reporting is activity description. A high yield from a single document usually means you have started accepting outputs.
5. **Split anything containing "and".** Two acts, or two actors, is two statements more often than not.
6. **Flag the gaps rather than filling them.** A change with no date gets flagged for the informant round, never a guessed date.

## Drafting from interviews

Never ask "what outcomes have you achieved". You get the objectives read back to you.

- Open with a time frame and a behaviour question. "Think about the last six months. Is anybody doing something differently now that they were not doing before? Who? When did you first notice?"
- Then probe in a fixed order: what exactly did they do, when, where, how do you know, who else saw it, what did we have to do with it, who else was pushing the same way.
- **Ask for the negatives explicitly and more than once.** "What has gone backwards?" "Has anything we did make things harder for anyone?" "What did you expect to happen that has not?" Informants self-censor. One polite question does not get past it.
- **Ask about actors outside the direct partners.** "Who else has changed what they do because of something a partner did?" This is the question that finds second-order outcomes. It is also the one most often skipped.
- **Ask about surprises.** "What happened that nobody planned for?"
- **Write the draft statement in front of the informant and read it back.** Correction on the spot costs a minute. Correction by email costs two weeks and often never arrives.

## The statement itself

Four parts in the description, plus two further fields that are part of the record and not part of the description.

**The description.**

- **Who changed.** The social actor, named specifically enough to find. "The Kisumu County planning department", never "local government".
- **What changed.** The act, in the active voice, in the past tense.
- **When and where.** A date or a bounded period, and a place or an organisational location.
- **Why it matters.** The significance, stated in terms of what the change makes possible or prevents, judged against the harvest users' concerns. One or two sentences. This is the field that gets padded; keep it to the consequence.

**The two further fields.**

- **Contribution.** What the programme is said to have done, **and who says so**. This is a claim at drafting time and stays a claim until something else is done to it. See [substantiation](outcome-harvesting-substantiation.md#the-contribution-claim).
- **Source and evidence.** Document reference or informant, plus the verbatim wording the statement rests on.

Some practitioners fold significance and contribution into a three-part statement. The slicing matters less than keeping contribution structurally separate from the description, so that the change can survive if the contribution claim collapses.

## Worked rewrites

The most useful thing on this page. Each shows the diagnosis, because the diagnosis is what transfers to the next case.

---

**Weak:** "Increased awareness of climate risk among local government."

*Diagnosis:* no named actor, no act (awareness is a state), no date, nothing anybody could confirm.

**Usable:** "In March 2025 the Kisumu County planning department added a flood-risk screening question to its capital project appraisal template, and applied it to the four projects appraised between March and August 2025."

*Significance:* capital projects in the county's flood-prone wards are now screened before approval, which had not previously happened at appraisal stage.

---

**Weak:** "Improved coordination between agencies."

*Diagnosis:* nominalisation hiding the actors. Whose coordination, doing what?

**Usable:** "From January 2025 the four agencies in the district WASH cluster have held monthly joint planning meetings, chaired in rotation, and have produced a shared quarterly plan for two consecutive quarters."

*Note the fields this makes available:* four actors or one (settle it), a date, a repetition count that supports the `repeated` flag, and a document (the shared plan) that substantiates it without needing anybody to reply to an email.

---

**Weak:** "The Ministry committed to reviewing the policy."

*Diagnosis:* a commitment is an intention. It may never happen. A set full of commitments inflates the count with things that never occurred.

**Usable, if the record supports it:** "In June 2025 the Ministry's Permanent Secretary issued a written instruction establishing a review committee for the 2018 policy, naming five members and a September reporting date."

That is an act with a document behind it. If no such instruction exists, drop the statement and note the expected review as a pending change in the analysis notes. Do not promote an intention by rewording it.

---

**Weak:** "Our training built the capacity of 40 CSOs."

*Diagnosis:* two faults at once. The actor is the programme, which makes this an output. And "capacity" is an inferred state.

**Usable:** "Between May and November 2025, 11 of the 40 CSOs that attended the March 2025 training submitted funding proposals using the logframe format taught on it. Four of the 11 were funded. The remaining 29 have submitted no proposals in the period."

*Diagnosis of the rewrite:* the actor is now the CSOs, the act is submission, the date is bounded. Note that this version also carries its own denominator, because the 40 attendees are a set somebody covered exhaustively. That is rare and valuable. See [the denominator problem](outcome-harvesting-analysis.md#the-denominator-problem).

---

**Weak:** "The programme influenced the national inclusive education strategy."

*Diagnosis:* a contribution claim wearing an outcome's clothes. There is no actor and no specified change, only an assertion about the programme.

**Split into two.**

*Outcome:* "The Ministry of Education's 2025 National Inclusive Education Strategy, published in April 2025, requires schools to report out-of-school children disaggregated by disability status. The 2020 strategy contained no such requirement."

*Contribution claim:* "Programme staff drafted this wording and submitted it to the strategy drafting committee in November 2024. A programme officer states it was adopted with minor edits. Not yet substantiated with a committee member."

Keeping these apart is the discipline of the method. The outcome stands whether or not the contribution claim survives.

---

**Weak:** "Beneficiaries reported feeling more confident."

*Diagnosis:* self-reported internal state, no actor, no act.

**Usable, only if the evidence contains it:** "Fourteen of the 22 women in the Nakuru savings group have opened individual bank accounts since the group formed in March 2024. None held an account before."

**If the evidence does not contain anything like that, drop the statement.** You cannot rewrite your way to an outcome the data does not hold. Rewriting is clarification. The moment it starts adding facts to satisfy the four-part test, the harvest has begun manufacturing its own findings. Under deadline pressure this happens often.

---

**Weak:** "Partners are now more willing to share data."

*Diagnosis:* willingness is a disposition. Also plural actors with no names.

**Usable:** "Between February and July 2025, three of the seven consortium members (A, B and C) supplied monthly caseload data to the shared dashboard. Before February none had supplied data to any shared system. The other four have not supplied data."

Note the negative half is carried in the same statement. Carrying the non-adopters alongside the adopters is what stops the set reading as seven out of seven.

## Fields to record at drafting time

Recording these while drafting costs seconds. Reconstructing them afterwards costs days. Some cannot be reconstructed at all.

| Field | Why it has to be captured now |
|---|---|
| `outcome_statement` | The description, four parts |
| `actor_name`, `actor_type` | Analysis groups on these. Free text alone will not group |
| `change_type` | Formal rule, practice, relationship, resource, discourse |
| `date_of_change` | Not the date of reporting. Substantiation and sequencing both need it |
| `location` | Organisational or geographic |
| `already_happened` | The intention filter, applied at drafting and checkable afterwards |
| `direction` | Positive, negative, mixed, against the harvest users' stated intent |
| `intended` | Separate axis from direction |
| `order` | First, second, third order relative to the programme |
| `persistence` | One-off, repeated, institutionalised |
| `contribution_claim` | What the programme is said to have done |
| `claimed_by` | Who says so. A claim with no claimant is unusable |
| `other_causes_named` | What else the informant credited |
| `source_ref` | Document or informant |
| `quote` | The verbatim wording. Everything else is derived from it |
| `possible_substantiator` | Who could confirm this, decided now |

## As a plan

A code step draws every fact on this page: the coder is handed the coding prompt and the column definitions and reads neither this page nor any other, so the four-part test, the categories under "what counts" and "what does not count", and the worked rewrites' diagnoses have to be compressed into the prompt and into each column's `means`, `counts` and `does_not_count` directly.

The four parts of the description sit most naturally as free-text columns (who changed, what changed, when and where, why it matters), since the wording is specific to each statement and a nominal or ordinal column cannot hold a sentence. The fields in the table above split the other way: `change_type` (formal rule, practice, relationship, resource, discourse), `order`, `persistence`, `direction` and `intended` are closed sets with a handful of values, so they are nominal or ordinal columns whose `values` spell out each one's `means` and `does_not_count`, borrowed straight from "what counts" and "what does not count" above. A column with a fixed, closed set of values, unlike a free-text one, can carry a second coder of another model family and an adjudicator that rules on anything the two coders place differently, so a disputed `order` or `direction` gets a second reading and a ruling with its reason; a disputed wording in the free-text actor or change description does not, because no type on offer holds free text from two readings and chooses between them. There is also no column type for a date as such, so `date_of_change` has to be coded as free text and stays outside that same cross-check, worth saying plainly to an evaluator who wants the dates checked twice.

Nothing in the engine splits a statement that names two acts or two actors, or flags a missing field rather than guessing it: both are instructions written into the coding prompt, not behaviour the engine enforces. Giving a column such as `date_of_change` or `order` an explicit value for "not stated" or "unclear" among its declared `values`, rather than leaving the coder to invent something plausible, is what keeps a gap in the material visible as a gap in the table instead of becoming an unflagged guess. The quote locator does enforce the fourth leg of the test mechanically: every quotation is matched against the document's own words, and one it cannot find is dropped rather than kept, so a row that survives always has a verifiable source.

Setting `check` on the code step (off by default, and under test) is the nearest the engine comes to rereading a sample of drafts against the test: a second model reads each coded passage in the text around it and keeps, recodes or drops it with its reason, which is where a statement that looks complete but describes a state rather than an act, the commonest fault on this page, gets caught. It is off by default and under test, so a workflow that wants it has to ask for it rather than assume it runs.
