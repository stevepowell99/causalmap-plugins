# Generic evaluation questions

What to do when a terms of reference asks for a verdict on effectiveness, impact, coherence, efficiency, sustainability, relevance, equity or adaptability, and the evidence is reports, minutes and interviews rather than a comparison group. Six of these are the revised OECD-DAC criteria, defined by the DAC in 2019 (OECD DAC 2019, pp. 7 to 12); equity and adaptability are not DAC criteria, but terms of reference ask for them often, and their pages say what grounds them. Unlike the causal questions answered by contribution analysis, process tracing or outcome harvesting, they need evaluative reasoning: criteria of merit stated openly, standards of performance a reader can observe, and a synthesis of scattered qualitative evidence into a verdict somebody can argue with (Scriven 1991; Davidson 2005). Applied to a corpus of project reports, interview transcripts, working group minutes and field audits, a model left without structure tends to write a speculative summary that overstates how common something is, drops the minority view and supplies a standard of its own.

The approach here keeps describing and valuing apart: coding against definitions that each say what they mean, a tabulation counted over a stated base, and a standard the evaluator supplies, applied in the open by a judge step. Nothing enforces that boundary, so keeping the valuing in the evaluator's standard, rather than inside a column's definition, is the workflow's design to get right. Without an external control group to lean on, each workflow compares within the corpus on the attributes the documents carry: what implementers claim against what beneficiaries report, or the milestones committed at inception against the timelines actually reached.

The DAC's own principles for use say the criteria should be "applied thoughtfully" and "contextualized", and that their use "depends on the purpose of the evaluation" rather than being applied mechanistically (OECD DAC 2019, p. 6). So a terms of reference that lists all six does not oblige a workflow for each; the evaluator chooses which the evaluation needs, and the 2021 guidance discusses how (OECD 2021, pp. 24 to 29).

## Why these questions need a rubric

Generic evaluation questions in a programme's terms of reference are normative rather than purely empirical: they ask for a judgement of merit or worth, which is what Scriven means by evaluation (Scriven 1991). In the logic of evaluation Scriven set out and Davidson turned into working method, a descriptive finding ("what occurred?") never yields an evaluative conclusion ("was it good?") on its own (Davidson 2005). An evaluative claim needs three things.

- **Criteria of merit.** The dimensions of the intervention that matter for the evaluation, such as administrative timeliness, external deconfliction or post-project recurrent financing.
- **Standards of merit.** Thresholds a reader can observe, saying what excellent, adequate or poor performance looks like in practice for each criterion.
- **Synthesis rules.** A stated rule for bringing the separate criteria together into an overall judgement, which does not let an average hide a failure in one place (Davidson 2005).

A workflow keeps the three apart. Leaving the verdict to a model without a stated standard gives ratings that nobody can reproduce, because the model supplies its own standard, changes it from call to call, and never says what it was. Here the evaluator supplies the standards and the rule for combining them, the judge step states them, and code applies them in the open. Write the workflow, standard included, before any document is coded, so that a reader can see the standard was not chosen to fit the counts.

A code step finds passages and records them against the column definitions; a tabulate step counts over stated bases; and a judge step applies the standard. A criterion whose standard is a threshold is a `rule`, settled by the count; every other criterion is a `reading`, decided by weighing the coded rows against the written standard, and the workflow states which each criterion is.

## Comparing within the corpus

Evaluations of development and public sector programmes often work from qualitative material without a comparison group or a baseline; the OECD guidance names missing baselines and disaggregated data among the challenges of judging effectiveness (OECD 2021, p. 55). Project files cannot show what would have happened without the programme. What they can offer is a comparison inside the corpus, on the attributes attached to each document.

| Comparison | The two sides | What it can show |
|---|---|---|
| Stakeholder role | Implementer and donor accounts against direct beneficiary testimony, on a role attribute | Self-serving reporting, frictions in the field nobody acknowledged, and different views of how good or useful the service was ([evidence quality rubrics](evidence-quality-rubrics.md)) |
| Phase | Design commitments at inception against field reporting later on, on a phase attribute | Fidelity to the design, milestone slippage, drift in the goals nobody managed, and adaptation under pressure |
| Administrative unit | Districts doing well against districts lagging, on a district attribute | The institutional and environmental conditions behind performance, and bottlenecks in particular places ([realist evaluation](realist-evaluation.md)) |
| Stance and governance | Internal administrative records against external oversight audits, on a genre attribute | Uncoordinated operations, hidden administrative overhead, duplication between agencies, and friction between policies |

A comparison on a document attribute puts each document wholly on one side, because a document is one case. A report mixing implementer and beneficiary voices, or a focus group spanning several cohorts, sits on one side of the table whatever it contains. A column on each coded row recording who is speaking, or which cohort a passage is about, is the way round that, which is why the equity page disaggregates by a recorded cohort as well as by a gender attribute.

## What the workflow holds to, and what it does not

Every criterion workflow depends on four things.

- **Every column and value says what it means.** Each value's definition states what it means, what counts and what does not, so that another coder would place the same passage the same way. A coder choosing between undefined labels is consistent only by chance. Where a passage sits close to a boundary, the row says so.
- **A base that keeps the documents that said nothing.** A proportion taken only over the passages that discussed an issue overstates it. A tabulation states what it counts (documents or rows), under which values, out of which base, and split by which column; its base is every document read unless it names another, so silence or non-delivery counts as a finding rather than dropping out. A count of rows counts what the coding returned rather than how many separate things there were, so a prevalence across documents is a count of documents, not of rows.
- **A trail back to the words.** Every quotation is checked against its document, every number in the answer is a count some tabulation holds, and every claim points at a row. A criterion about the method rather than the material, such as whether enough documents yielded anything, is answered from the workflow and its tabulations rather than by citing text.
- **A rule for combining that does not average.** A programme is often only as strong as its weakest link, and averaging ordinal scores across criteria hides the weak link (Davidson 2005). A judge step's `combine` reads worst-first (`weakest`), where the weakest criterion decides, as when technical sustainability needs institutional and financial viability together, or best-first (`best`), where the better of two routes to the same outcome carries the claim, or reports each criterion `separate`ly, or follows the evaluator's own `rules`; never an average. The five workflows tabulated below read worst-first; the effectiveness page combines objectives separately unless the evaluator says how much each matters.

Nothing in a tabulation tests whether a difference between groups could be chance. The [equity page](generic-evaluation-equity-and-differential-reach.md#whether-a-difference-could-be-chance) says when a test would mean anything.

## As a plan

**A sample or code step says which documents it reads, and the workflow's reasons say why those documents.** A stratified sample where the question compares groups, the whole corpus otherwise, since kinds grouped from a sample hold only what that sample raised.

**A criterion is a `rule` or a `reading`, and the judge step says which before any table is counted.** A rule tests a tabulation against a threshold, which code applies without argument: more than a stated number of documents describing an unmitigated barrier, more than a stated number of unfinanced pillars. A reading weighs the coded rows against a descriptor for each level, citing the rows it relied on and those against. A criterion is never left as both, because a threshold chosen after the count is seen is a threshold chosen to fit the answer. Every level is the evaluator's, marked `from: evaluator` with their words, or `from: proposed` and put to them before anything is read.

**Where a column does not describe a passage, the row still needs a value for it.** A column that only bears on some passages gets an explicit value for the rest, with its own definition, rather than being left blank.

**The coding prompt searches on the material's own words, and several phrasings of the subject, never on the verdict it is testing for.** The coder sees only the prompt and the column definitions, so this is the one place a search that would otherwise confirm whatever it was looking for gets caught, before any document is read.

**Group open codes into kinds, and say the counts are floors.** Where a column is free text, group its labels into kinds, define each kind, and record each row's kind as a nominal column (or one binary column per kind where a row may carry several). An open coder names the most obvious things and stops, so counts from an open coding are floors.

**A verdict's name travels further than its reasoning.** Under a worst-first combine the overall verdict is named for whichever criterion failed hardest, so the write step's instructions should say to name it for what actually failed rather than for the criterion's own generic label, exactly as the relevance page's own naming problem shows.

**Without the evaluator's standard, there is no judge step.** The workflow stops at the tables and says under `cannot_answer` that the verdict waits on the evaluator's standard, offering two or three standards in their terms without numbers.

**Agreeing the standard, and making sense of the verdict, are done with people.** What good looks like on each criterion, how much each objective matters and what a verdict means for the programme are normally settled with the commissioner, programme staff and, where possible, the people the programme serves; a workflow over documents cannot stand in for that, so it proposes candidate criteria, levels and definitions, and reads what an earlier agreement left in the documents where it is there. A Rubicon run can stop at agreed points for people to take part, once the definitions are written and a few documents coded so that stakeholders can agree what each category means before everything is counted, and once the answer is drafted, and its report is a draft to make sense of with the people it concerns before it is final.

These hold across all the criterion pages and are stated here once rather than on each.

## Across five of the criteria

Effectiveness and impact are set out on their own pages.

| Criterion | What is counted | Contrast inside the corpus | Failure it guards against | How the criteria combine |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coherence | Documents recording formal synergy against those recording friction or duplication, internal and external | Internal strategic directives against external sector working group records | Coordination forum inflation: meeting attendance taken for operational synergy | Worst-first, with a single document recording friction or duplication never going unremarked: internal policy alignment cannot make up for duplication in the field |
| Efficiency | Documents reporting a critical delay, by domain and by partner | Inception workplans against later progress reports, disaggregated by partner | The disbursement illusion: full budget spend taken for delivery on time | Worst-first: speed of spending cannot outweigh delays that leave outputs unused |
| Sustainability | Documents describing operations secured on domestic funding against those foreseeing a stop, by pillar | Implementer handover reports against the recurrent budget allocations local authorities describe | The aspirational commitment trap: political support taken for recurrent financing | Worst-first, with more than a handful of documents foreseeing an unfinanced stop deciding the verdict: ownership cannot run a system nobody funds |
| Relevance | Congruence among the passages about beneficiaries, and adaptation by year | Community needs assessments against what the project logframe delivered | The static inception fallacy: relevance at design praised while the project failed to change course after a shock | Worst-first: relevance at the start cannot excuse delivery that the context had made obsolete |
| Equity | Mitigation and benefit counted by cohort, and the share of barriers mitigated, each against its cohort's own base | Testimony from majority participants against testimony from vulnerable cohorts | Aggregate masking: a high overall count of participants while vulnerable cohorts meet elite capture | Worst-first, with the verdict held at tokenistic once more than a handful of barriers are left unmitigated: a high aggregate cannot outweigh systematic exclusion of the groups the programme targeted |

The names in the fourth column are this page's own labels for common faults, not terms from the literature. The thresholds in the fifth are examples of standards an evaluator might give; none applies until the evaluator gives it.

Each plan states the documents it read, counts over a stated base, compares within the corpus on the documents' own attributes, and keeps every number traceable to the words, which is what lets an evaluator answer these criteria and a reader check the answer.

The DAC itself noted that, although the criteria were never mandatory, in practice "they can end up being applied mechanistically" (OECD DAC 2019, p. 3). These plans answer them with counts split by the corpus's own attributes that lead back to quotations. Each verdict comes after a stated reading, counts over a stated base, a comparison inside the corpus and a standard stated in the workflow, which leaves a model less room to supply a standard of its own. What the evaluator and the commissioner get is a trail from each verdict to the passages behind it.
