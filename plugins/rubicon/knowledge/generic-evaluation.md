# Generic evaluation questions

What to do when a terms of reference asks for a verdict on effectiveness, impact, coherence, efficiency, sustainability, relevance, equity or adaptability, and the evidence is reports, minutes and interviews rather than a comparison group. These are the revised OECD-DAC criteria of effectiveness, impact, coherence, efficiency, sustainability and relevance, with the cross-cutting requirements for equity and dynamic adaptability that most terms of reference add [1]. Unlike the causal questions answered by contribution analysis, process tracing or outcome harvesting, they need evaluative reasoning: criteria of merit stated openly, standards of performance a reader can observe, and a synthesis of scattered qualitative evidence into a verdict somebody can argue with [4]. Applied to a corpus of project reports, interview transcripts, working group minutes and field audits, a model left without structure tends to write a speculative summary that overstates how common something is, drops the minority view and supplies a standard of its own [4].

Rubicon keeps describing and valuing apart: a reading against a codebook whose every item says what it means, a tabulation counted over a stated base, and a standard the evaluator supplies, held in a judge step and applied in the open [4]. Nothing in the engine polices that boundary, so keeping the valuing in the judge step's standard, rather than inside a codebook item, is the workflow design's to get right, and the write step's review to catch [4]. Without an external control group to lean on, each workflow compares within the corpus on the columns the documents carry: what implementers claim against what beneficiaries report, or the milestones committed at inception against the timelines actually reached [4].

## Why these questions need a rubric

Generic evaluation questions in a programme's terms of reference are normative rather than purely empirical: they ask for an assessment of worth, merit and justification [3]. In Michael Scriven's logic of evaluation, which Julian King has put into practice at length, a descriptive finding ("what occurred?") never yields an evaluative conclusion ("was it good?") on its own [3]. An evaluative claim needs three things.

- **Criteria of merit.** The dimensions of the intervention that matter for the evaluation, such as administrative timeliness, external deconfliction or post-project recurrent financing [3].
- **Standards of merit.** Thresholds a reader can observe, saying what excellent, adequate or poor performance looks like in practice for each criterion [4].
- **Synthesis rules.** A stated rule for bringing the separate criteria together into an overall judgement, which does not let an average hide a failure in one place [3].

Rubicon keeps the three apart, and every step's output is kept [4]. Leaving the verdict to a model without a stated standard gives ratings that nobody can reproduce, because the model supplies its own standard, changes it from call to call, and never says what it was [4]. In Rubicon the evaluator supplies the standards and the rule for combining them, the judge step states them before any table is read, and the write step applies them in the open [4]. What the record does not yet show is whether a standard was fixed before anybody read the material.

A code step finds passages and records them against the codebook; a tabulate step counts over stated bases; and a judge step applies the standard [4]. A criterion whose standard is a threshold is a rule, settled by the count; every other criterion is a reading, decided by weighing the coded rows against the written standard, and the workflow states which each criterion is [4].

## Comparing within the corpus

Evaluations of development and public sector programmes often work from qualitative material without a comparison group or an experimental baseline [5]. Project files cannot show what would have happened without the programme [4]. What they can offer is a comparison inside the corpus, on the columns attached to each source when it was imported [4].

| Comparison | The two sides | What it can show |
|---|---|---|
| Stakeholder role | Implementer and donor accounts against direct beneficiary testimony, on a role column | Self-serving reporting, frictions in the field nobody acknowledged, and different views of how good or useful the service was [7] |
| Phase | Design commitments at inception against field reporting later on, on a phase column | Fidelity to the design, milestone slippage, drift in the goals nobody managed, and adaptation under pressure [9] |
| Administrative unit | Districts doing well against districts lagging, on a district column | The institutional and environmental conditions behind performance, and bottlenecks in particular places [13] |
| Stance and governance | Internal administrative records against external oversight audits, on a genre column | Uncoordinated operations, hidden administrative overhead, duplication between agencies, and friction between policies [9] |

A comparison on a source column puts each document wholly on one side, because a case is one document by default [4]. A report mixing implementer and beneficiary voices, or a focus group spanning several cohorts, sits on one side of the table whatever it contains. A column on each coded row recording who is speaking, or which cohort a passage is about, is the way round that, which is why the equity page disaggregates by a recorded cohort as well as by a gender column.

## What the workflow holds to, and what it does not

Every criterion workflow depends on four things the pieces do.

- **Every item says what it means.** A group step's codebook states, for every item, what it means, what counts and what does not, and which documents it was built on; the check refuses an item that leaves one of these out [4]. A model choosing between undefined labels is consistent only by chance [4].
- **A base that keeps the documents that said nothing.** A proportion taken only over the passages that discussed an issue overstates it [4]. A tabulate cell states what it counts (documents or rows), under which values, out of which base, and split by which column; its base is every document read unless it names another, so silence or non-delivery counts as a finding rather than dropping out [4]. A count of rows counts what the coding returned rather than how many separate things there were, so a prevalence across documents is a count of documents, not of rows.
- **A trail back to the words.** A quotation goes through the quote locator, which checks it against its source and snaps it to its exact position where the match is exact, canonical or gapped; a fuzzy match is kept with approximate offsets and labelled as such; a quotation that cannot be found is dropped, and the run's record says how many [4]. Every number in the answer is a cited count, and every claim points at a row. A criterion about the method rather than the material, such as whether enough documents yielded anything, is answered from the record rather than by citing text [4].
- **A rule for combining that does not average.** A programme is often only as strong as its weakest link, and averaging ordinal scores across criteria hides the weak link [5]. A judge step's combine setting reads worst-first (`weakest`), where the weakest criterion decides, as when technical sustainability needs institutional and financial viability together, or best-first (`best`), where the better of two routes to the same outcome carries the claim; never an average [4]. The setting is stated in the workflow and applied by code. The five workflows tabulated below read worst-first; the effectiveness page combines objectives separately unless the evaluator says how much each matters.

Nothing in a tabulation tests whether a difference between groups could be chance. The [equity page](generic-evaluation-equity-and-differential-reach.md#whether-a-difference-could-be-chance) says when a test would mean anything.

## As a plan

**A sample or code step says which documents it reads, and the workflow's reasons say why those documents.** A stratified sample where the question compares groups, the whole corpus otherwise, since a codebook built from a sample holds only what that sample raised.

**A criterion is a rule or a reading, and the judge step says which before any table is counted.** A rule tests a tabulation against a threshold, which code applies without argument: more than a stated number of unmitigated barriers, more than a stated number of unfinanced pillars. A reading has a strong model weigh the coded rows against a descriptor for each level, citing the rows it relied on and those against. A criterion is never left as both, because a threshold chosen after the count is seen is a threshold chosen to fit the answer.

**Where a column does not describe a passage, the row still needs a value for it, since every declared column is filled from its own declared type.** A field that only bears on some passages gets an explicit value for the rest, with its own `means`, rather than being left blank.

**The coding prompt searches on the material's own words, and several phrasings of the subject, never on the verdict it is testing for.** The coder sees only the prompt and the column definitions, so this is the one place a search that would otherwise confirm whatever it was looking for gets caught, before any document is read.

**Cost is spent in the group and write steps, which read every row or every document rather than a sample.** A write step can also be handed the documents in full (`documents`, off by default); measured on test questions this bought no accuracy and added about 60% to the write step's cost, so it is left off unless a specific document has to be read whole.

**A verdict's name travels further than its reasoning.** Under a worst-first combine the overall verdict is named for whichever criterion failed hardest, so the write step's instructions should say to name it for what actually failed rather than for the criterion's own generic label, exactly as the relevance page's own naming problem shows.

These hold across all the criterion pages and are stated here once rather than on each.

## Across five of the criteria

Effectiveness and impact are set out on their own pages.

| Criterion | What is counted | Contrast inside the corpus | Failure it guards against | How the criteria combine |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coherence | Passages at formal synergy against passages at friction or duplication, internal and external [9] | Internal strategic directives against external sector working group records [9] | Coordination forum inflation: meeting attendance taken for operational synergy [9] | Worst-first, with a single passage of friction or duplication never going unremarked: internal policy alignment cannot make up for duplication in the field [4] |
| Efficiency | The share of coded passages reporting a critical delay, by domain and by partner [9] | Inception workplans against later progress reports, disaggregated by partner [9] | The disbursement illusion: full budget spend taken for delivery on time [9] | Worst-first: speed of spending cannot outweigh delays that leave outputs unused [4] |
| Sustainability | Passages describing operations secured on domestic funding against those foreseeing a stop, by pillar [1] | Implementer handover reports against the recurrent budget allocations local authorities describe [9] | The aspirational commitment trap: political support taken for recurrent financing [9] | Worst-first, with more than a handful of passages foreseeing an unfinanced stop deciding the verdict: ownership cannot run a system nobody funds [4] |
| Relevance | Deep congruence among the passages about beneficiaries, and adaptation by year [1] | Community needs assessments against what the project logframe delivered [4] | The static inception fallacy: relevance at design praised while the project failed to change course after a shock [9] | Worst-first: relevance at the start cannot excuse delivery that the context had made obsolete [4] |
| Equity | Mitigation and benefit counted by cohort, and the share of barriers mitigated, each against its cohort's own base [12] | Testimony from majority participants against testimony from vulnerable cohorts [12] | Aggregate masking: a high overall count of participants while vulnerable cohorts meet elite capture [9] | Worst-first, with the verdict held at tokenistic once more than a handful of barriers are left unmitigated: a high aggregate cannot outweigh systematic exclusion of the groups the programme targeted [4] |

Each plan states the documents it read, counts over a stated base, compares within the corpus on the sources' own columns, and keeps every number traceable to the words, which is what lets an evaluator answer these criteria and a reader check the answer [4].

Generic criteria are often answered with a narrative summary nobody queries, written to a reporting deadline [1]. These plans answer them with counts split by the corpus's own columns that lead back to quotations [4]. Each verdict comes after a stated reading, counts over a stated base, a comparison inside the corpus and a standard stated in the plan, which leaves a model less room to supply a standard of its own [4]. What the evaluator and the commissioner get is a trail from each verdict to the passages behind it, which joins qualitative depth to the accountability a public evaluation owes [3].
