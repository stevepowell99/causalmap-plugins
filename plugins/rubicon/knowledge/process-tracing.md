# Process tracing

Process tracing asks whether a hypothesised causal mechanism actually operated inside one case, by working out in advance what traces each part of the mechanism should have left and then going to look. It is a within-case method. It says nothing about what would happen elsewhere.

Subsidiary pages: [mechanisms and predictions](process-tracing-mechanisms.md), [the evidence tests](process-tracing-evidence-tests.md), [in Rubicon](process-tracing-in-rubicon.md).

## What it establishes

A finished process tracing supports a claim of the form: the mechanism ran, or part of it did, or it did not, in this case, and here is the evidence that discriminates between those. The inference is about a particular case and rests on the evidence being more likely under one account than under its rivals.

That last clause is the whole method. A piece of evidence is worth something only where it would have been unlikely had the hypothesis been false. Everything else in process tracing is machinery for making that judgement explicit and checkable.

Four variants, following Beach and Pedersen's second edition. **Theory-testing** starts from a mechanism somebody has already articulated and asks whether it ran here, and it splits in two: a **minimalist** form asking only whether the mechanism was present, and an **in-depth** form tracing each part of it. **Theory-building** works from the evidence towards a mechanism nobody had stated. **Explaining-outcome** aims at a sufficient explanation of one particular result, borrowing from wherever it has to, with no ambition to generalise. Evaluation work is mostly theory-testing and explaining-outcome.

The first edition (2013) had three, theory-testing undivided. A source saying "the three variants of process tracing" is citing that edition, and the split matters here: minimalist theory-testing is what a corpus of many short accounts can support, while in-depth theory-testing is what a single well-documented case can.

What it does not establish: an effect size, a claim about other cases, or a counterfactual magnitude. It also cannot rescue a hypothesis too vague to yield predictions, which is the condition most programme theories are in when somebody first asks for process tracing.

## When it fits, and when it does not

It fits where the question is how something happened rather than whether it generally does. It fits where a mechanism can be broken into parts, each with something it should have left behind. It fits where that residue plausibly exists: minutes, drafts, emails, budget lines, people who were there and remember. And it fits where there is time to go back to the sources repeatedly, because tracing is iterative by nature.

The second half.

**A mechanism nobody can state in parts.** "Capacity building improved services" is a black box, and a black box yields no predictions, so there is nothing to test. The work of decomposing it is the work. Where the programme cannot or will not do that, process tracing has not started. Offer the decomposition as the first deliverable: it is a workshop with the programme rather than an analysis task.

**Evidence that cannot exist.** If the discriminating trace would have been a decision nobody minuted, taken by people who have left, then the test cannot be run whatever the design says. Establish that the evidence could exist before promising the method.

**Only retrospective accounts.** "Nobody wrote anything down at the time, but the team remember it well." Retrospective accounts from people with an interest are weak on uniqueness, so most tests come out as straws in the wind. Say what the method could establish on that material, which is usually much less than the commissioner expects, before agreeing to it.

**A question about generalisation.** Process tracing gives one case. Somebody asking whether the programme would work in the next district wants something else, and a comparative design is what to offer, or tracing in two or three cases chosen to differ in a way that matters, presented as exactly that.

**Twelve cases in a thirty-day contract.** Process tracing is expensive per case. The expense sits in the reading and the going back rather than in anything that scales. Promising it across a portfolio is the commonest way it gets agreed and the commonest way it fails. Two or three cases done properly beat twelve done as narrative.

**A hypothesis nobody is willing to lose.** The method only works where the answer could come out no. Where the commissioner has already decided the programme worked and wants the mechanism written up, what they want is a case study. Calling it process tracing adds a claim to rigour the work will not carry.

## What data it needs

Contemporaneous material above all. A document written at the time, by somebody with no reason to angle it for an evaluator, is worth more than any number of retrospective accounts. Its probative value comes from having been produced for another purpose entirely.

Quantity matters less than in most methods. One minute of one meeting can be decisive where it records a decision the rival explanation says was never taken. So the sampling logic is search rather than draw. You are looking for particular things you named in advance, and reading everything is often unnecessary.

Provenance has to be recorded per source, since who produced a document and why is part of what makes its presence probative. A project completion report claiming the mechanism operated is weak evidence that it did.

## The steps in practice

1. State the outcome to be explained, in the particular case, precisely enough that somebody could disagree about whether it occurred.
2. Articulate the mechanism as a sequence of parts, each an entity doing something.
3. For each part, write the observable manifestations it should have left if it operated, and what should be absent or present if it did not.
4. Name the rival explanations, and write their predicted manifestations too.
5. Assess each prediction for certainty and uniqueness, which is what determines the kind of test it is.
6. Register all of the above before searching.
7. Search for each prediction, and for each rival's predictions, with the same effort.
8. Record what was found, what was looked for and not found, and where you looked.
9. Update: say what each test did to confidence in the mechanism and in each rival.
10. State the conclusion at the confidence the tests support, and say what would have changed it.

Steps 2 to 6 are most of the intellectual work and all of the method's claim to rigour. Step 8's middle clause is the one people skip.

## Who does what

The evaluator articulates the mechanism, writes the predictions, judges certainty and uniqueness, and decides what the evidence did to confidence. None of that is mechanical. None of it can be delegated to a procedure.

The programme or the commissioner supplies the theory of change and should agree the mechanism and the predictions before the search. Their agreement is what stops the mechanism being redrawn later to fit whatever turned up. A workflow over documents cannot give that agreement or run the decomposition workshop: it can draft the mechanism and predictions from the programme's own documents for them to argue with, and a Rubicon run can stop once the definitions are written and a few documents coded so they agree the register before anything is counted.

## Traps

Each comes with a test somebody can apply to a draft. Nearly all of them produce a document that reads like good process tracing, which is why the tests are worth having.

- **Predictions written after the evidence.** The central failure, and the one the method exists to prevent. Test: is there a dated register predating the search? Does the report include the predictions that failed? A register containing only successful tests was either extraordinarily well designed or edited afterwards.
- **The mechanism is a black box.** "Capacity building improved services" with test labels attached. Test: can you name each part as an entity doing something? Could each part be false while its neighbours are true?
- **Rivals named and never tested.** A paragraph acknowledging that other factors existed. Test: does each rival have its own predictions? Were they searched for with the same effort as the hypothesis?
- **Test labels applied post hoc.** Evidence turns up and is then christened a smoking gun. Test: for each label, is there an argument about certainty and uniqueness against the named rivals, written before the search?
- **Absence of evidence read as evidence of absence.** A failed hoop reported as decisive. Test: does the report say where the search ran? Does it give a reason for expecting the trace to exist and survive?
- **Never-searched collapsed into searched-and-absent.** Test: can a reader tell, for each prediction, whether anybody looked?
- **Everything is a smoking gun.** Test: count them. Smoking guns are rare. A register where most predictions are highly unique has usually confused striking with discriminating.
- **Evidence consistent with the rivals reported as support.** The document confirms the mechanism operated and also confirms the trend was already under way. Test: for each piece of evidence, is it more likely under the hypothesis than under every named rival? Where it is not, it does not discriminate, whatever it shows.
- **Provenance ignored.** A project completion report cited as evidence that the project's mechanism worked. Test: who produced each source, when, for what audience? Contemporaneous material produced for another purpose outranks anything written for an evaluator.
- **Correlated evidence counted as independent.** Three interviews from one office, or three documents drafted by the same person. Test: would these have come out differently if the first were wrong? Where they would not, they are one piece of evidence.
- **Bayesian numbers with invented priors.** A posterior of 0.87 resting on a prior somebody chose. Test: where does the prior come from? Does the conclusion change if it moves a reasonable amount? If it does, report the reasoning rather than the number.
- **Narrative substituted for test.** A coherent, well-evidenced account that never risked coming out the other way. Test: name the evidence that, had it been found, would have overturned the conclusion. If there is none, no test was run.
- **Confidence overstated in the conclusion.** Straws in the wind summing to a strong claim. Test: does the stated confidence match the tests actually passed? Is the strongest evidence against the conclusion reported?

## What good looks like

The mechanism appears as named parts rather than as a story. Each part carries predictions written before the search, and a reader can see they were. Rivals are stated as specific accounts with their own predictions rather than named in a sentence and dropped. Each piece of evidence carries an argument about why finding it was unlikely under the rivals, which is what makes it probative. A label asserting that something is a smoking gun does none of that work. Evidence looked for and not found is reported with an account of where the search ran. And the conclusion is stated at a confidence the tests support, with a sentence saying what would have changed it.

A process tracing that can be assessed contains: the outcome to be explained, stated precisely; the mechanism as named parts; the rivals as specific accounts; the register of predictions with its dates and versions, including the ones that failed and the ones abandoned; for each prediction, the certainty and uniqueness argument; what was found, with quotations and provenance; what was searched for and not found, with the search described; what each result did to confidence; the conclusion at a stated confidence; the strongest evidence against it; and what would have changed it. The two items people cut are the failed predictions and the search descriptions. Those two carry the method's whole claim to have tested anything.

A process tracing whose predictions cannot be shown to predate the evidence is a case study with test names attached.

## Combining it with other methods

Contribution analysis is close kin and often the better fit for evaluation work, because it accommodates a mechanism running alongside other causes rather than requiring the analyst to adjudicate between rivals. Process tracing is the better instrument where rivals really do compete.
