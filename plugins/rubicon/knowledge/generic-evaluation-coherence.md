# Generic evaluation questions: coherence

A plan for judging internal coherence, with the agency's own policies and programmes, and external coherence, with government, other donors and civil society, from reports, minutes and partner interviews. It guards against the commonest fault in coherence findings, taking attendance at coordination meetings for synergy.

Back to [generic evaluation questions](generic-evaluation.md), for what the criterion pages share and for the works cited.

## Scope, and the contrast inside the corpus

The 2019 revision added coherence as a criterion, asking "How well does the intervention fit?" and defining it as "The compatibility of the intervention with other interventions in a country, sector or institution" (OECD DAC 2019, p. 8). Its note splits it into two domains (OECD DAC 2019, p. 8).

- **Internal coherence** "addresses the synergies and interlinkages between the intervention and other interventions carried out by the same institution/government, as well as the consistency of the intervention with the relevant international norms and standards to which that institution/government adheres". Those norms include commitments such as human rights, gender equality and environmental safeguards.
- **External coherence** "considers the consistency of the intervention with other actors' interventions in the same context", including "complementarity, harmonisation and co-ordination with others, and the extent to which the intervention is adding value while avoiding duplication of effort". In practice that means partner governments, bilateral donors and civil society organisations working in the same sector or place, and whether the project adds something distinct or builds parallel structures.

The 2021 guidance treats coherence at length and sets out the challenges of evaluating it (OECD 2021, pp. 45 to 51, Table 4.2 on p. 48).

The main trap in a qualitative coherence evaluation is taking the absence of conflict for synergy. Interventions can run in separate silos, avoiding open confrontation with other actors while duplicating supply chains, setting up competing local committees or bypassing existing municipal structures. The plan looks for those tensions by contrasting the implementing agency's own records with external sector reviews and partner interviews, on the attribute that says which stakeholder group each document comes from.

## As a plan

**Sample.** Every document: the strategic and coordination documents, partner interviews and government assessments. Where the corpus is too large to read whole, stratify by the stakeholder group attribute, so both sides of the contrast are read.

**Code.** A nominal column for the domain a passage concerns (internal, external), and an ordinal column for the interaction, from `friction_or_duplication` through `isolated_coexistence` and `active_coordination` to `programmatic_synergy`. Each level's definition says what counts and what does not: attendance at a coordination meeting is `isolated_coexistence` at most, `active_coordination` needs joint planning or shared information acted on, and `programmatic_synergy` needs text showing pooled resources, joint delivery or formal data sharing. The whole point of the scale is to stop attendance being read as synergy, so define the levels tightly enough that another coder would place the same passage the same way; where a passage sits between attendance and coordination, the answer says the call was close. Where documents mix voices, such as a joint review written by the agency and the ministry together, add a column for whose account a passage gives. The coding prompt asks for friction and duplication to be searched for with the same effort in the agency's own records as in the partner and government material.

**Tabulate.** Count documents by interaction level and domain, and by interaction level and stakeholder group, so the `.within.` cells show, of the documents from each side, how many record friction or duplication. Counts are of documents, so two documents describing one duplicated service are two documents rather than two problems.

**Judge.** Only with the evaluator's standard. A typical one is a `rule`: the verdict is no better than adequate wherever even one document records friction or duplication, whatever the rest show, read worst-first so that internal policy alignment cannot make up for duplication in the field. Without a standard, leave the judge out and offer standards in their terms without numbers: coherent where partners outside the agency describe joint delivery, or where no partner describes duplication.

**Write.** Each domain in turn, the agency's account beside what partners and government say, every friction or duplication named with its documents, and the count read as how much of the material records friction, not as a count of separate frictions.

**What the workflow must say it cannot answer** (its `cannot_answer`).

- **Coherence with interventions the documents do not mention.** The plan reads what the corpus says about other actors; a parallel programme nobody wrote about does not appear.
- **Whether a reported synergy saved money or effort**, unless the documents say so.

Try the level definitions on a handful of documents from each side of the contrast before coding the rest, mainly to catch a level worded broadly enough that nothing could fail it.

## What it guards against

The main weakness of coherence evaluations is what might be called coordination inflation: a list of working group meetings attended accepted as proof of external coherence. The ordinal levels are the guard: attendance on its own does not reach active coordination, while programmatic synergy needs text showing pooled resources, joint delivery or formal data sharing. Counts split by stakeholder group put side by side what implementers say about coordination and what line ministries say about parallel administrative units, so a gap between the two is visible in the table. Read worst-first, strong internal policy compliance cannot make up for serious duplication of services in the field.

## Where Rubicon falls short

- **The contrast is between documents rather than speakers.** A count split by stakeholder group puts each document on the side its attribute names, so a joint review written by the agency and the ministry together counts as one of them. A column on each coded row naming who is speaking would split it.
- **A count of friction documents is not a count of frictions.** Two documents describing one duplicated service count twice. Read the count as how much of the material records friction, which is what the threshold is about, and not as how many separate problems there were.
