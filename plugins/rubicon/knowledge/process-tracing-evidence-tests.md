# Process tracing: the evidence tests

Process tracing's four evidence tests (straw in the wind, hoop, smoking gun, doubly decisive), the likelihood ratio that generates them, and the negative-evidence problem that decides most real inferences.

Back to [process tracing](process-tracing.md).

## Two properties, from which the four tests follow

Every piece of predicted evidence has two properties, and the four tests are just the four combinations.

**Certainty.** How sure we are the evidence would be found if the hypothesis is true. High certainty means the mechanism could hardly have run without leaving this.

**Uniqueness.** How unlikely the evidence would be if the hypothesis is false. High uniqueness means the rivals struggle to account for it.

|  | Low uniqueness | High uniqueness |
|---|---|---|
| **Low certainty** | Straw in the wind | Smoking gun |
| **High certainty** | Hoop | Doubly decisive |

**Straw in the wind.** Passing nudges confidence up, failing nudges it down. Neither necessary nor sufficient. Most evidence is this. A case built entirely from straws stays weak however many there are.

**Hoop.** Necessary, not sufficient. The hypothesis must jump through it. Failing kills the hypothesis; passing only keeps it alive. A hoop test is the cheapest way to eliminate a rival, which is why rivals are worth writing predictions for.

**Smoking gun.** Sufficient, not necessary. Passing confirms strongly; failing weakens the hypothesis only slightly. Rare. Worth a great deal where it exists.

**Doubly decisive.** Necessary and sufficient: passing confirms and failing kills. Almost never available in social settings. A claim to have found one deserves scepticism.

The names are a convenience. What does the work is the argument about certainty and uniqueness for that piece of evidence in that case, which is why [the register](process-tracing-mechanisms.md#predictions-which-are-the-product) asks for the argument rather than the label. A label applied after the evidence turned up is decoration.

## The tests are a coarse version of one ratio

Behind the four boxes sits a single quantity: how much more likely the evidence is under the hypothesis than under its rivals. Certainty is the probability of the evidence given the hypothesis. Uniqueness is one minus its probability given the rivals. The ratio of the first probability to the second, the likelihood ratio, is what updates belief. The four tests are that ratio rounded to four corners.

Two consequences worth holding onto even where nobody writes a number down.

**Probative value is relative to the rivals you specified.** The same document is a smoking gun against one rival and a straw in the wind against another. So evidence cannot be classified in the abstract. A register naming its rivals is the only place classification means anything.

**Weak evidence accumulates, and it accumulates multiplicatively.** Several independent straws can move a conclusion further than one hoop. Independence is the condition, and it is usually what fails: three documents from the same office are close to one piece of evidence.

Fairfield and Charman set out the explicit Bayesian version, and Befani and Stedman-Bryce have applied it in evaluation. Numbers are contested. The objection worth taking seriously is that invented priors produce false precision. The middle position most practitioners take: reason in likelihood ratios qualitatively, say which way each piece of evidence pushed and roughly how hard, and do not report a posterior probability to two decimal places on the strength of a guessed prior.

## Absence of evidence

This is where most real process tracing inferences are actually decided, and it is the part that goes wrong most often.

A hoop test fails when the predicted evidence is not there. That failure is decisive only if three things hold. The evidence would certainly have been created if the mechanism ran. It would have survived to now. And somebody actually looked for it where it would be.

Miss any one of those and an absence means nothing. A minute never taken, a file destroyed, and a search that read the wrong folder all produce the same empty result. The report cannot tell them apart unless the search is described.

So a negative finding has to carry its search: where you looked, what you looked for, how much of the material you read, and why you expected the evidence to be there. Without that account a failed hoop test is a gap in the reading, reported as a finding.

Three practical rules.

**Say what would have created the trace.** "If managers had taken this decision it would appear in the minutes, because every management decision in this period appears in the minutes." That sentence is what makes the absence informative. It is a claim about the record-keeping rather than about the programme, so it can be checked on its own.

**Report the search at the same detail as the finding.** A found piece of evidence gets a quotation and a citation. An absence should get the equivalent: the sources read, the terms searched, the coverage.

**Distinguish never-searched from searched-and-absent.** These are different results. A register that collapses them is worthless for every test depending on absence. Coding the prediction as a column over the sample's documents keeps the two apart: a document outside the sample carries no row, while one inside it that the coder found wanting is coded absent and counted among those read, so the two cannot be confused; a code step's record says which part of a document it read, so a result read from only part of it says so.

## Reporting the update

For each prediction, say what was found, what test it was and why, and what it did to confidence in the hypothesis and in each rival. Then say where the case stands overall, at a confidence the tests support.

Two habits worth keeping. State the strongest evidence against your conclusion, since a reader will otherwise assume you did not look. And end with what would have changed your mind. It is the cheapest sentence in the report and the one that most distinguishes a test from a story.
