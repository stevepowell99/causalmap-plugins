# Contribution analysis

Setting out the theory of change a programme rests on, gathering the evidence for and against each of its links, taking the rival explanations seriously, and assembling what survives into a contribution story. John Mayne's method.

## What it establishes

That a programme plausibly made a difference, given the evidence and given what else was happening, with the reasoning laid out link by link so a reader can disagree with a particular part of it.

That is the whole licence. Contribution analysis establishes a **reduction in uncertainty about a causal claim**, not a share of the outcome. Mayne's own language is "plausible association" (a phrase he takes from Hendricks) and reducing uncertainty about the contribution (Mayne 2001).

The unit of the method is the **link**: one proposition from the theory of change, capable of being false on its own.

## When it fits

- A counterfactual is impossible, unethical or unaffordable, and the programme still has to say whether it worked.
- A theory of change exists, or can be reconstructed, and somebody is willing to commit to it in writing before the analysis.
- The programme works through other actors, so its own delivery data cannot answer the question.
- The commissioner will act on knowing which part of the chain is weak, which is what this method is best at and least often asked for.
- Existing evidence has to carry the analysis, because there is no budget for a new round of data collection. Mayne's third step assumes exactly this.

## When it does not fit

- **The commissioner wants a number for the programme's share.** Refuse at inception, in writing. This is the expectation that curdles at the end of the job.
- **There is no theory of change and nobody will agree one.** Reconstructing it from documents alone produces a link-by-link map of the evaluator's imagination.
- **The theory of change is a logframe with forty nodes.** Testing all of them is neither possible nor useful. Take the one or two central mechanisms and say that is what was tested.
- **The outcome is contested rather than the contribution.** Where people disagree about whether the change happened at all, that is a measurement question and comes first.
- **Only implementers are available to interview.** The method can run, and the contribution story then rests entirely on people paid to believe it.
- **The intervention has not happened yet.** Contribution analysis asks whether something that happened made a difference. Asked of a proposed programme, it becomes an appraisal of how plausible the theory is, and the evidence is analogy: testimony about another agency, elsewhere, at another time. Say so in the reading of the question, and make the analogy's transfer a link of its own ("what worked there works here, under these conditions"), since every other link rests on it and it is the one a sceptic attacks first.

## What data it needs

Documents and interviews bearing on each link, from people on more than one side of the programme. Nothing about the method requires new fieldwork, which is why it is affordable and also why it is often thin.

Two properties of the corpus matter more than its size. Whether it contains anybody with a reason to doubt the programme, and whether it was gathered before or after the theory of change was written. A corpus gathered beforehand cannot have had the theory put to it, which caps what any analysis of it can claim.

## The steps in practice

Mayne's six iterative steps (ILAC Brief 16, 2008), several of which he says can be run participatively. Marked *participatory* below are the three where a workflow cannot stand in for the people involved.

1. **Set out the attribution problem.** What causal question is actually being asked, and what would count as an answer.
2. **Develop the theory of change, and the risks to it.** *Participatory.* A theory of change nobody outside the evaluation team recognises is worthless. This step also names the rival explanations, and they are best drawn from people with a reason to doubt.
3. **Gather the existing evidence** on each link.
4. **Assemble the contribution story and the challenges to it.** The weak links are the product here, not an embarrassment.
5. **Seek additional evidence** where the story is thin. *Participatory, and the step a retrospective analysis cannot do.*
6. **Revise and strengthen the story.** *Participatory.* Sense-making with the people who will use it.

## Traps

- **A list of propositions is not a theory of change.** A need, three design features and a budget are five claims, not a chain. The links have to share their ends, from the intervention through its intermediate results to the outcome, and each carries the assumptions it makes and the outside influences on it (Mayne's well-developed theory of change, with its results chain, assumptions, risks and rival explanations). A theory whose links do not connect cannot be failed by a weak link, which is the property the method exists to use.
- **A rival that stands caps the link it competes with.** A link claiming the programme did what would not otherwise have happened cannot be better supported than its counterfactual rival is refuted. Where "it would have happened anyway" is supported and nothing contradicts it, the link is at best contested, however many people praise the programme.
- **Rivals written as straw men.** A rival is worth searching for only if a reasonable sceptic holds it. Draw rivals from the sceptics' own arguments, not from the programme's account turned upside down: for a research funder, for instance, that its successes came from a customer that would have bought the work anyway, and that its successes are remembered and its failures are not.
- **One proposition per passage.** A coding told to put each passage under the single proposition it bears on most directly makes links and rivals compete for passages, so a passage that supports a link and a rival counts for one of them only. Let a passage carry every proposition it bears on, and give the rivals their own pass.

- **Two kinds of rival get conflated.** A rival to a link (the training did not raise accuracy, the new form did) is local. A rival to the whole outcome (it would have happened anyway, another programme did it, the indicator is an artefact, the improving sites were already improving) is not. Teams test the first and write up as though they had dealt with the second. A theory of change every link of which is well evidenced is still consistent with the programme having contributed nothing.
- **Averaging a chain.** If the theory runs A to B to C to D and nothing bears on B to C, the story fails at B to C. It does not score three out of four. The natural summary of a table is a total, so the combination rule has to be written down before the results arrive, and it has to be closer to a minimum than a mean. So the answer reads the chain worst-first. Qualified by branches: a weak link in one branch is not fatal where another route reaches the outcome.
- **Reading silence as refutation, or as support.** A link nobody mentioned has three readings: too obvious to remark on, never asked about, or it did not happen. Only the third is a finding about the programme. Silence in a sample of ten documents out of a hundred is silence in the sample, and says nothing about what the evidence as a whole never examined.
- **Losing the links nobody mentioned.** Build the results table from what was found and the untouched links vanish, which is the failure the method exists to prevent. The full list of links has to be declared in advance so an untouched one reads as a nought.
- **Implementer testimony as evidence about participants.** An implementer's account of why participants responded is the implementer's theory, and it is data about the implementer.
- **The contribution story as the deliverable.** It reads well and cannot be queried. The annotated theory of change is the thing somebody can act on.
- **A verdict nobody asked for.** Where the commissioner asks for the annotated theory of change and says no overall verdict, give none: a chain verdict is the line that gets quoted, and it carries none of the qualifications the links do.
- **A standard that promises more than it tests.** "At least three supporters, and supporters outnumber contradictors two to one" applied as "at least three supporters" is the first half only, so a link with twelve for and six against comes out well supported. Apply both halves, from counts of the cases for and against each link.
- **A write-up that reaches its own statuses.** Whoever writes the annotated theory of change works from the link judgements. Given only the codings and the counts, the writer decides each link's status afresh, and the report then carries two verdicts per link.
- **Counting passages and calling them sources.** "Touched by 99 passages from 99 sources" in a run that read nine is the number's name telling a story its count does not. Count the cases behind a link, never its passages.

## What good looks like

- The attribution question is stated, and the questions that were asked but not answered are stated too, with the reason.
- The theory of change is in the report as a list of propositions, each one capable of being false.
- Every link carries its evidence for, its evidence against, and its status where nothing bears on it.
- The rival explanations for the whole outcome are named, searched for with the same effort as the programme's own account, and reported whether or not they were found.
- The combination rule is stated and was stated before the results.
- The weak links are prominent rather than buried, because they are what the reader can act on.

## As a plan

A contribution question can be read three ways, and a workflow states which one it used rather than letting the choice pass silently.

**Direct links.** One link per outcome, the programme straight to the change, counted as the documents that join the two: a code step with a column for what each passage credits, closed to the programme and its named rivals, and a tabulate step over that column. This is cheapest and loses every intermediate step the programme might have worked through.

**A given chain.** The theory of change becomes the values of a nominal column, one value per link, each `means` stating the proposition, drawn from the theory-of-change document or workshop note. A code step with that column reads the whole corpus once against the whole list; a link nobody's material touches still gets a cell, at nought, in the tabulate step that follows, rather than dropping out of the answer, because every value the column allows is a cell whether or not a row filled it. The rival explanations for the whole outcome get a nominal column of their own, read by a separate code step over the same documents at the same depth as the links, the same prompt structure, the same sections, because a rival searched less hard than the programme's own account inflates every link's support by default. The write step states the annotated theory of change itself, one line per link citing the tabulation cell behind it, combined the way a chain combines: the least-supported link decides the pathway's overall standing, in the write step's own words, and a link contested by a well-supported rival is named as contested however many rows support it. Its known fault is the one causal mapping's own pages call the transitivity trap: a link evidenced in one document and the next link evidenced in another are two separate claims, and reading the chain as proven end to end treats two people's accounts as one person's story.

**An open map, traced.** A code step with two free-text columns, cause and effect, reads for causal claims without a fixed list; the labels at both ends are then grouped into one factor vocabulary, each factor defined, as [causal mapping in Rubicon](causal-mapping-in-rubicon.md) sets out, and a second, closed code step codes cause and effect against that one vocabulary. A tabulate step with `paths` set, by cause and effect, traces every path through each document's own links and counts the documents whose links make it, so a path from the intervention to the outcome is only ever joined within one source's account, never stitched from several people's separate claims. Its rows are the links on the path, so the answer can read whether the source told it as one chain or the coder's links merely meet; the transitivity trap is narrowed to within one account rather than closed. The other influences on the outcome are the paths into it from anything other than the programme, counted the same way.

Whether an account is first-hand or prompted varies from passage to passage, so it is a coded column; the speaker's relation to the programme is a document attribute where each document has one speaker. A tabulate step can split a count by either, and a story resting only on paid staff shows as such in the table rather than staying implicit in the prose, whichever of the three readings above is used.

The commonest misreading is a passage taken as supporting a link when it in fact supports the rival. Guard against it in the definitions: give each link and each rival what counts and the near misses that go to the other, tightly enough that another careful coder would place the same passage the same way, and where a passage could be read either way, say so in its row and, where a link's standing turns on it, in the answer. Where a workflow uses the given chain, the write step takes both the links' tabulation and the rival's as inputs, so the rival's support is read with the same attention as the programme's own. Running the workflow on two or three documents against one or two links before the rest is where a link written so broadly that no passage could fail it gets caught cheaply.

None of the three readings does the method's participatory steps. A workflow cannot develop the theory of change and its rivals with stakeholders (step two), go back to people for additional evidence (step five) or revise the story with those who will use it (step six); it reads a theory of change agreed elsewhere where that is among the documents, and otherwise drafts candidate links, rivals and definitions, an open map traced included, for people to agree. A Rubicon run can stop at agreed points for people to take part, once the links and rivals are written as definitions and a few documents coded so stakeholders can agree what each one means before everything is counted, and once the answer is drafted, and its annotated theory of change is a draft to make sense of with them before it is final.
