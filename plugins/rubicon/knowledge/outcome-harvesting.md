# Outcome harvesting

Working backwards from observed changes to what the intervention had to do with them. Developed by Ricardo Wilson-Grau with colleagues, drawing on Outcome Mapping's framing of behaviour change in actors the programme does not control.

## What it establishes

That specified changes in the behaviour of named social actors happened, when and where they happened, and what people say the intervention had to do with each of them.

That is the whole licence. An outcome harvest establishes occurrence, with dates, actors and corroboration. It does not establish causation, prevalence, magnitude or value for money. Every one of those gets read into a harvest report by commissioners unless the report refuses them explicitly.

The unit of the method is the **outcome statement**: one social actor, one change in what they do, dated and placed, with a note of why it matters and a record of who claims the programme contributed.

## When it fits

- Outcomes could not be specified in advance. Advocacy, policy influence, network building, research uptake, systems work, capacity support, grant-making with wide grantee discretion.
- The intervention works through other people's decisions. It funds, convenes, trains, drafts or brokers, and somebody else acts.
- The commissioner wants to know what actually changed rather than whether targets were met.
- Many actors across many places, one small evaluation team, no baseline and no comparison group.
- The theory of change is suspected of being incomplete. Finding the unpredicted changes is part of the job.
- Retrospective work where nobody set up monitoring at the start. OH is one of the few methods that tolerates this.

## When it does not fit

Be blunt with the commissioner about all of these, in the inception report, before any data collection.

- **The question is attribution or effect size.** There is no counterfactual anywhere in the method and no way to add one afterwards. A harvest that is asked "did the programme cause this" answers "several people say so", which is a different sentence.
- **The commissioner needs prevalence or coverage numbers.** An outcome set has no natural denominator (see [analysis](outcome-harvesting-analysis.md)). Percentages out of a harvest describe the harvest.
- **Outcomes are already known, countable and monitored.** Then OH is an expensive way to reproduce the management information system. It will also produce a smaller number than the MIS, because harvesting is a search.
- **Standard service delivery with defined beneficiaries.** Behaviour change in a controlled delivery chain is not what the method is built for.
- **The budget cannot carry substantiation.** An unsubstantiated harvest is a list of programme claims. Readers treat it that way and they are right to. If substantiation is being cut, say the deliverable has changed from an evaluation to a documented set of programme claims. Put that on the cover.
- **The programme will not tolerate negatives.** Without negative and unintended outcomes the set is promotional material with a method attached.
- **The timeline is short.** Substantiation cannot be compressed: it depends on other people replying. For anything above about thirty outcomes, allow eight weeks minimum from first draft to analysed set.
- **It is wanted as a routine performance instrument.** Once outcome counts become the thing staff are judged on, drafting inflates within one reporting cycle and negatives stop appearing. OH survives as periodic evaluation and degrades as a KPI.

There is also a common half-fit worth naming. A commissioner asks a causal question and has an emergent, multi-actor programme. OH is right for the results half and cannot do the causal half. The workable answer is a harvest that produces the verified outcome set, followed by contribution analysis or process tracing on a handful of selected outcomes, costed as a second phase with its own data collection. See [combining with other methods](outcome-harvesting-analysis.md#combining-with-other-methods).

The conversation worth rehearsing happens late and under pressure: a commissioner reads the draft and asks for an attribution statement, a percentage, or a comparison against another programme. The answer is the same in all three cases. Name what the harvest establishes, name what the question needs, and offer the specific extra work that would close the gap, with a cost: contribution analysis on the whole programme, process tracing on two selected outcomes, or a census over a bounded frame, agreed now and covered exhaustively. Softening it does not help anybody. A sentence written to satisfy a commissioner that the evidence does not support is the sentence a peer reviewer or a sceptical board member finds first, and it costs more later than the argument costs now.

## What data it needs

**Documents.** Progress and grant reports, partner reports, minutes, back-to-office notes, email threads, media coverage, published strategies and gazettes, MEL data. Expect a low yield: a long annual report often gives two or three usable outcomes, because programme reporting describes activity. Documentary evidence is strongest for the fact of a formal change (a policy, a budget line, a published document) and weakest for practice change inside an organisation, which is rarely written down anywhere.

**Informants.** Programme staff, direct partners, and, more important than either, actors one or two removes out: the people the partners influenced. Second and third order outcomes are where OH earns its cost, and they are invisible from inside the programme.

**Substantiators.** People with direct knowledge of a specific outcome who do not depend on the programme. Identify them while drafting. Leaving it until step 4 means going back through every source to work out who could confirm what.

**Dates.** A statement with no date cannot be substantiated (the substantiator has nothing to place), cannot be sequenced, and cannot be counted per period. Undated statements are the largest single source of waste in a harvest.

What has to be true: informants can name specific actors and specific changes rather than talking in themes; you can reach people outside the programme; and the programme has run long enough for behaviour change to have occurred, which for practice change in organisations is rarely under about eighteen months, though single advocacy wins can appear much sooner.

## The steps in practice

The canonical sequence is six steps. In practice steps 2 and 3 interleave, and step 1 is the one everybody shortens and should not.

1. **Design the harvest.** *With people.* Name the harvest users, the people who will act on the findings. Agree the questions they need answered, in writing. Then settle four things that are near impossible to fix later: what counts as an outcome for this harvest (the actor boundary, whether internal programme changes are in scope, the period covered); the fields every statement will carry; the **denominator sets** you will need at analysis time and will therefore have to cover exhaustively; and the substantiation rule, which sample and how many. Half the [traps](#traps) are step 1 omissions surfacing at step 5.

2. **Review documentation and draft outcome statements.** Read a defined document set, drafting candidate statements as you go with the source reference attached at the moment of drafting. Do not read everything and then write. See [identifying](outcome-harvesting-identifying.md).

3. **Engage the change agents.** *With people.* Programme staff and partners review the drafts, correct them, and add what the documents missed. Most of the yield comes from this step, and most of the quality problems too, because staff cannot see their own outputs as outputs. Run the first round as a facilitated workshop if the budget allows; remote drafting produces activity lists. See [coverage and who harvests](outcome-harvesting-coverage.md).

4. **Substantiate.** *With people.* Independent informed sources confirm, correct or dispute a sample of statements. This is the step that converts programme claims into evaluation findings. It is also the step that gets cut. See [substantiation](outcome-harvesting-substantiation.md).

5. **Analyse and interpret.** Classify, group into trajectories, count against declared denominators, and answer the step 1 questions. The interpreting is done with the harvest users. See [analysis](outcome-harvesting-analysis.md).

6. **Support use.** *With people.* The outcome table in full as an annex, a small number of trajectories in the body, and a stated account of what was searched and who was not asked.

**Who does what.** The harvester (usually the evaluator) designs, drafts, edits and pushes back. Change agents supply and correct. Their contribution claims remain claims. Substantiators verify and never see the programme's own attribution before they answer. Harvest users set the questions in step 1 and are the reason the harvest has a boundary at all. A harvest with no named user drifts into collecting everything and answers nothing anybody asked.

## Traps

Each comes with a test somebody can apply to a draft in a few minutes. Most trace back to a decision skipped when the harvest was designed. The first four are the ones that ruin studies.

- **Activity lists dressed as outcomes.** The most common by a wide margin, and it survives being corrected twice. Test: for each statement, is there a named actor outside the programme, an active past-tense verb, and a date? Count the failures. More than one in ten means the drafting round has to be rerun rather than patched.
- **No negatives in the set.** If nothing bad was harvested, the search failed. Test: how many harvested outcomes are negative, mixed, or ran against the programme's intent? Treat a wholly positive set as a finding about the method, and say so in the report.
- **Contribution claims written up as findings.** The fault that most damages an evaluation's standing, because a careful reader spots it immediately. Test whether the sentence survives having "according to the programme" added to it. If it does not, it was a claim.
- **Percentages with no denominator, or with "outcomes harvested" as the denominator.** Every reader converts these into prevalence. Say the base in the same sentence, every time, and make it a set somebody covered exhaustively. See [the denominator problem](outcome-harvesting-analysis.md#the-denominator-problem).
- **Substantiation skipped, thin, or done by friends.** Test: for each substantiated outcome, name the substantiator's relationship to the programme. Count how many are funded by it, employed by it, or partnered with it. Then check the report says how many.
- **Merging that hides chains.** A causal sequence collapsed into one statement loses the information anybody actually wanted. Test: look for statements containing "leading to", "which resulted in", "cascaded", or naming two actors. Each is a candidate for splitting into two outcomes with a recorded relationship.
- **Retrofitting to the logframe.** Sorting outcomes into predicted boxes and dropping the leftovers removes the one thing the method offers. Test: how many outcomes in the final set were unpredicted by the theory of change? If the answer is near zero, either the theory was unusually good or the leftovers were discarded. Ask which.
- **Undated statements.** Test: count statements with no date or an unusable one ("during the project"). Anything above a handful means the drafting instructions did not enforce it.
- **Counting statements rather than actors.** One talkative partner outvotes five reticent ones. Test: recompute the headline numbers by distinct actor. If the story changes, the actor version is the one to report.
- **Stopping when informants ran out.** Test: does the report state a stopping rule decided in advance, and what was left undone? See [deciding when to stop](outcome-harvesting-coverage.md#deciding-when-to-stop).
- **Answering an attribution question anyway.** A commissioner asked whether the programme caused the change, the harvest cannot say, and the draft says it in careful language that reads as a yes. Test: search for "contributed to", "led to", "as a result of", "helped bring about". For each, is there an independent substantiator, and has any alternative explanation been tested? If neither, the sentence needs rewriting as a claim with a claimant.

A good outcome set can still produce a poor report.

- **Outcome counts presented as performance.** Ten outcomes this year against six last year is not improvement, and putting the two numbers side by side asserts that it is.
- **No coverage paragraph.** Without it, a reader cannot weigh anything. The [template is here](outcome-harvesting-coverage.md#the-coverage-paragraph).
- **Significance judged by the harvester's feel.** If the report ranks outcomes by importance, the criteria have to be written down and agreed with the harvest users before the ranking is made.
- **Trajectories replaced by category counts.** A bar chart of change types is less use than four dated narratives with named actors. Commissioners read the narratives.
- **Limitations written as boilerplate.** State the specific things this harvest cannot support, named, in the body where the reader meets the relevant claim.

## What good looks like

A reader judging a harvest should be able to check all of these from the report and its annexes.

- Every statement names a social actor, an act, a date and a place, and could be confirmed by somebody who was there.
- The set contains negative, unintended and disappointing outcomes. Where it does not, the report explains what was done to look for them.
- Contribution appears throughout as an attributed claim, with the claimant named, and never as an unmarked finding.
- The substantiation rule is stated (which outcomes, why those, how many), the response rate is reported, and disagreements between substantiators survive into the analysis instead of being averaged away.
- Every percentage names its base in the same sentence. That base is a set somebody covered exhaustively.
- The report states what was searched, over what period, and who was not asked.
- The complete outcome table ships as an annex, including statements that were dropped and the reason.
- Any change quoted in the body can be traced back to a document or a named interview. Commissioners check this. It is usually the first thing a sceptical reader tests.

A reporting structure that works:

- **Body.** The questions the harvest users set, answered directly. Three to six change trajectories with dates and named actors. Where the evidence is thin, said in the same paragraph as the claim rather than in a limitations section nobody reaches.
- **Coverage paragraph** in the methods section.
- **Annex A: the complete outcome table.** One row per outcome, carrying actor, change, date, location, significance, contribution claim with claimant, source, substantiation status and substantiator relationship.
- **Annex B: dropped statements** with reasons. This annex is what convinces a careful reader that the set was filtered on a rule rather than on preference.
- **Annex C: the harvest design** as agreed at the start, including the definitions used and the substantiation rule.

## As a plan

Full detail: [outcome harvesting in Rubicon](outcome-harvesting-in-rubicon.md#as-a-plan). In brief.

Drafting and substantiation (steps two to four) stay outside the six pieces: they are judgements made by people who were there, corrected and confirmed by other people, and nothing in sample, code, group, tabulate, judge or write runs a human round or waits on a reply. What a workflow does is code statements once they exist as text, or draft candidate statements from documents for people to correct. A code step reads the documents one row per quotation, and the four-part description (actor, change, significance, contribution claim with its claimant) is written as declared columns. The coder sees only the coding prompt and the column definitions, so the method's tests have to be written there in full. A harvest workflow can stop at its tables, with no judge step, because the harvested statements are the answer rather than a verdict; `cannot_answer` says so for any part a judgement would need a standard it has not been given.

The same holds for the steps done with harvest users: agreeing the questions and what counts as an outcome (step one), interpreting the findings (step five) and using them (step six). A workflow cannot stand in for any of them; it reads what an agreed harvest design says where that is among the documents, and otherwise drafts candidate definitions, tables and quotations for the harvest users to correct. A Rubicon run can stop at agreed points for people to take part, once the definitions are written and a few documents coded so the harvest users can agree what each category means before everything is counted, and once the answer is drafted, and its report is a draft to interpret with them before it is final.

Analysis (step five) is where grouping and tabulating earn their place. Group the free-text change descriptions into kinds of change, define each kind, and record each row's kind as a nominal column; counts from an open coding are floors. A tabulate step counts the coded statements by any column or document attribute, every cell stated out of the documents or rows it was taken from. Tabulate counts documents or rows, not distinct actors, so a reach claim such as "17 actors changed" is not a number a workflow produces by itself.

The traps translate into what the coding prompt and each column's definition say outright: the active-verb-and-date test, and the rule that a contribution claim carries its claimant. Write the definitions tightly enough that another coder would place the same passage the same way, and where a placement is close, say so in the row. Proving the search looked for negatives takes a second, differently worded code step over the same documents: tabulate each, and have the write step cite both, so a wholly positive set is reported as a finding about the search rather than passed over.

A trial classifies a handful of already-drafted outcomes before the rest, which is where a classification value too broad to fail gets caught cheaply.
