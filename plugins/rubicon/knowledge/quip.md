# QuIP: the Qualitative Impact Protocol

Read this when a question names QuIP, asks for self-reported attribution, or brings transcripts from blindfolded interviews: QuIP asks people what changed in their lives and why, without naming the programme being evaluated, then codes their causal claims to see whether and how they credit it.

Related pages: [causal mapping](causal-mapping.md), the coding and analysis QuIP data is usually put through; [contribution analysis](contribution-analysis.md), whose theory of change QuIP evidence is often read against; [generative and counterfactual causation](generative-and-counterfactual.md), for what a self-reported attribution is evidence of.

## What it establishes

QuIP establishes whether the people a programme meant to reach credit it, unprompted, for changes in their lives, and how its effect sits among everything else they credit. James Copestake and colleagues at the University of Bath developed it as a way of getting credible evidence on attribution without a control group, and Bath Social & Development Research has applied it widely since; the case book (Copestake, Morsink and Remnant, 2019) collects its early uses.

Its distinctive claim is about bias. Asking people about a programme invites them to praise it, out of courtesy, hope of more support, or the plain suggestion in the question. QuIP blindfolds the interview: field researchers independent of the programme ask about changes in domains of the respondent's life over a recall period, and why they happened, without naming the programme or its commissioner. A respondent who then credits the programme, or one of its activities, does so without being led. That makes each attribution a stronger piece of evidence than the same words given in answer to "how did the project help you?".

What QuIP does not establish: the size of an effect, the share of an outcome due to the programme, or how common an experience is in the population. Samples are small and purposive. What it gives is a set of accounts in which the programme's contribution, its absence and its rivals are all visible, case by case, which is evidence for or against the theory of change rather than an estimate.

## When it fits, and when it does not

It fits where the programme's intended beneficiaries can perceive and describe the changes it aimed at, within a recall period they can remember; where a theory of change exists to compare their accounts against; and where independent interviewers can be found who do not know, or are not told, what is being evaluated.

It does not fit, or fits only partly, in four situations.

**Changes beneficiaries cannot see.** A programme that worked upstream, on policy, institutions or supply chains, may change people's lives through routes they cannot trace. They will credit what they can see. QuIP will then under-credit the programme, and the report has to say so.

**Blinding that cannot hold.** In a small community with one prominent programme, respondents may guess what the interview is about. Blinding is then partial, and the analysis treats an explicit mention of the programme with more caution. It is still worth doing: an interview about the respondent's life rather than about the project changes what people say.

**A commissioner who wants a number.** QuIP's counts are of respondents in a purposive sample. Reported as percentages of a population they mislead. Refuse at inception.

**No theory to compare against.** QuIP can run without one, as an open inquiry into what drives change, but its use as evidence of contribution depends on knowing what the programme did and what it expected to happen, so that an account can be recognised as crediting it without naming it.

## What data it needs

Interview transcripts, and often focus group notes, from a purposive sample of intended beneficiaries: usually a few dozen interviews per study (the case book's benchmark for a single QuIP is 24 interviews and four focus groups), selected to cover the variation that matters (places, types of participant, and where monitoring data exists, people whose outcomes went well and people whose did not). A list of the programme's activities and its theory of change, held by the analyst and not by the interviewers. Any attribute of the respondent the analysis will compare by.

## The steps in practice

1. **Design.** Agree the domains of change the interview will cover (income, food, health, relationships, whatever the theory of change bears on), the recall period, and the sample.
2. **Blindfold.** Commission independent field researchers, brief them on the domains but not the programme, and keep the commissioner out of the introduction to respondents.
3. **Interview.** Open questions about change in each domain, then why it happened, probing for causes without suggesting any.
4. **Code.** The analyst, now un-blinded, reads each account for causal claims (this caused that) and records, for each claim, its cause, its effect, whether the change was for better or worse, and its attribution code: explicit where the respondent attributes it to the programme or to a named programme activity, implicit where it confirms or refutes the mechanism in the programme's theory of change with no reference to the programme or its activities, or other where it is credited to something outside that theory (Table 1.1 of the case book sets out the full code set).
5. **Analyse.** Count respondents per claim and per kind of attribution; read the stories behind the most and least credited links; set the evidence against the theory of change link by link.
6. **Report and discuss** with the commissioner, including what respondents credited that the programme had not expected, and what the programme expected that nobody mentioned.

## Traps

- **Unblinding through the questions.** A domain written in the programme's vocabulary ("since the savings groups started") tells the respondent what is being evaluated. Test: could a respondent tell from the questions alone which programme commissioned them?
- **Implicit attribution read as explicit, or missed.** "We restocked as soon as we could borrow" describes the mechanism of a credit programme's theory of change without naming the programme, and only an analyst who knows the theory of change can tell. Test: is the line between explicit, implicit and other written down, with the theory of change and the programme's activity list beside it, before coding?
- **Counts read as prevalence.** "Fourteen of twenty-four respondents" is a finding about the sample. Test: does any sentence turn a count into a percentage of the population?
- **Only the programme's links reported.** The rival drivers respondents named are the evidence that makes the programme's share credible or not. Test: are other causes of the same outcomes counted on the same footing?
- **Negative change dropped.** Respondents report things getting worse, sometimes because of the programme. Test: is the direction coded, and are negative attributions reported?
- **Silence read as refutation.** A link in the theory of change that nobody mentions may be one nobody noticed rather than one that failed. Test: does the report say which of the theory's links the accounts never touched, and what that can and cannot mean?

## What good looks like

The report opens with what respondents credit: the programme's explicit and implicit attributions, reported separately, beside the other drivers of the same outcomes, positive and negative, with counts of respondents rather than of passages. The strongest unexpected findings are given space. The theory of change is annotated link by link with what the accounts support, contradict or never reach. Each causal claim used is traceable to the respondent who made it, and quotations are the respondents' words. How the sample was chosen, how blinding was done and where it was partial, and what the counts are out of, are stated after the findings, with the limits.

## As a plan

QuIP analysis is causal coding with one extra column, so most of [causal mapping](causal-mapping.md) applies.

**Sample.** Every transcript; a QuIP study is small enough to read whole.

**Code.** One code step over every transcript, a row per causal claim, with a free-text cause and a free-text effect, a nominal direction (better, worse), and a nominal attribution column whose values are explicit (the respondent names the programme or one of its activities), implicit (the change confirms or refutes the mechanism in the programme's theory of change, with no reference to the programme or its activities) and other (credited to something outside that theory). The definitions carry the theory of change and the programme's activity list in its own words, since the coder cannot tell implicit from other without them, and say that a claim counts only where the respondent states a cause, not where the coder infers one. Then group the cause and effect labels into one vocabulary of factors, each defined, recorded as nominal columns, so that the same outcome is counted the same way whoever describes it. Keep the attribution column even where the question does not mention attribution: it is what makes the analysis QuIP rather than a general causal coding, since without it the answer cannot say whether respondents credited the programme unprompted.

**Tabulate.** Count respondents (documents, where each transcript is one respondent) by outcome and attribution, so each outcome shows how many credit the programme explicitly, implicitly, or credit something else; by outcome and direction; and by cause and effect with `sparse` set for the links themselves. `paths` traces chains within each respondent's own account, never across respondents. Where respondents differ by an attribute that matters, add it.

**Judge.** Only where the evaluator gives a standard for what counts as evidence of contribution, for example a number of respondents crediting a link explicitly or implicitly with none crediting the reverse. See [evaluative rubrics](evaluative-rubrics.md).

**Write.** The answer leads with whether and how respondents credit the programme for the outcomes it aimed at, beside what else they credit; then the unexpected and the negative; then the theory of change link by link. Counts are of respondents in the sample. Explicit and implicit attribution are reported separately. How the transcripts were coded and what the counts are out of come last, with the limits, never before the findings.

**What stays with people.** Designing the study, agreeing the domains and the sample, the blindfolded fieldwork and the discussion of findings with the commissioner (steps one to three and six) happen outside any workflow, which reads only the transcripts and programme documents they produced. A Rubicon run can stop once the attribution definitions are written and a few transcripts coded, so the commissioner and the programme can agree where implicit ends and other begins before everything is counted, and once the answer is drafted, which is a draft for the step six discussion rather than its outcome.

**What the workflow must say it cannot answer.** The size of the programme's effect; how common any experience is beyond the sample; whether blinding held, which only the fieldwork record shows; and changes respondents could not perceive.
